Node vector analysis

Analysis of vectors defined on the nodes of a network (graph signals): generalized Euclidean distance, annihilation distance, earth mover’s distance, network correlation, variance and entropy, and the graph Fourier transform.

In multilayer networks, vectors are defined on the copies (entity, layer) of a component, and distances use the supra-Laplacian (see Laplacians): this is the multilayer generalized Euclidean distance (Coscia, 2022). An attribute given for a whole entity has its value on every copy; for the probability distributions of network_variance() and network_entropy(), this gives more mass to the entities present in more layers.

In hypergraphs and simplicial complexes, vectors are defined on the nodes, and all functions use the network of the random walk of Zhou et al. on a hypergraph (weight naming a hyperedge attribute), or the 1-skeleton of a complex, as the Laplacians do (see Laplacians). Signals on the edges, triangles and higher simplices of a complex have their own Hodge decomposition and Fourier transform (see Topological signals).

graphoskop.annihilation_distance(net, a, b, component=0, F=None, sparse=None, weight=None, alpha=0.0, normalize=True, tol=1e-10)

Annihilation distance between two node vectors (Coscia, Gomez-Lievano, McNerney and Neffke, 2020, section 3.1.3): the mass of a spreads by random walks with positive sign, the mass of b with negative sign, and wherever they meet they annihilate. After \(k\) steps the net mass is \(W^k (a - b)\), which vanishes as both reach the stationary distribution: the faster, the closer the vectors. The distance is the norm of the net mass summed over all steps,

\[\delta(a, b) = \| S \|_2, \quad S = \sum_{k \geq 0} W^k (a - b) = F (a - b),\]

with \(W\) the column stochastic transition matrix of the walk (\(W_{ji}\) the probability of a step from \(i\) to \(j\), i.e. \(E D^{-1}\) on undirected networks) and \(F = (I - W + W^\infty)^{-1}\) its fundamental matrix (see fundamental_matrix()), so \(\delta = \sqrt{(a - b)^T F^T F (a - b)}\). It is a metric on vectors of equal mass (the norm of an invertible linear map).

The paper writes the step as \(P a\) with the row stochastic \(P = D^{-1} E\); for the mass to move along the edges and \(P^k a\) to reach the stationary distribution (as the paper states), the step must be \(P^T a = W a\), which is used here (the two agree on regular networks). The series converges only if the two vectors have the same mass (otherwise the net mass tends to a multiple of the stationary distribution and the sum grows without bound), so they are rescaled by normalize as in earth_movers_distance(); and only on aperiodic walks: on periodic ones (bipartite networks without teleportation, e.g. a chain) the closed form is the Cesaro limit of the series. On undirected networks \((a - b)^T D^{-1} F (a - b)\) is exactly \((a - b)^T L^+ (a - b)\), the square of generalized_euclidean(): the annihilation distance weighs the same summed net mass \(S\) without dividing it by the degree. In the chain test of the paper (all the mass at one end, then at the other) it is not \(\sqrt{n - 1}\) (the generalized Euclidean distance) but grows faster: 4.0, 14.4 and 46.0 for chains of 5, 10 and 20 nodes.

\(F\) is used as given in F (from fundamental_matrix(); pass it to reuse it across many pairs of vectors), or computed densely (sparse = False), or \(S\) is obtained without any n by n matrix (sparse = True): on undirected networks without teleportation, exactly as \(S = D L^+ \delta - \pi \, d^T L^+ \delta\) (with \(\delta = a - b\), \(d\) the strengths and \(\pi = d / \sum_i d_i\)), with the conjugate gradient of laplacian_solver() on the device of the network; otherwise with GMRES on the CPU, which needs many iterations if the walk mixes slowly (long directed chains or cycles without teleportation). By default (sparse = None), dense up to 2,000 nodes, sparse above. On two CPU threads, sparse takes about 0.05 seconds on 5,000 nodes and 15,000 edges (0.4 seconds on a chain of 3,000 nodes) and 0.7 seconds on 100,000 nodes and 300,000 edges, dense 4 seconds on 5,000 nodes. The walk must have a unique stationary distribution (see fundamental_matrix()).

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • a – Node attribute (position in net.node_attributes or name), or vector of length n (numpy.ndarray or torch.Tensor), with non negative entries.

  • b – Second node vector, same modalities as a.

  • component – Index of the component (0 is the largest), or None for all components. With None, vectors and F are given as lists with one entry per component.

  • F – Cached fundamental matrix, or None.

  • sparse – Use GMRES (True) or the dense fundamental matrix (False) when F is None; None to choose by size.

  • weight – Name of the edge attribute used as edge weight, or None for unit weights.

  • alpha – Teleportation probability of the walk (needed on directed networks that are not strongly connected).

  • normalize – True (both to sum 1), "min" (both to the smaller sum), "max" (both to the larger sum) or False (sums already equal).

  • tol – Relative tolerance of GMRES.

Returns:

Distance, or list of distances if component is None; NaN if a vector has missing values.

Raises:

ValueError – If a vector has negative entries, the masses cannot be made equal, or the walk has no unique stationary distribution.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", "nodes.tsv")
d = gsk.annihilation_distance(net, "y2000", "y2010")
F = gsk.fundamental_matrix(net)
ds = [gsk.annihilation_distance(net, x, y, F = F) for x, y in pairs]   # cached
d = gsk.annihilation_distance(big, "y2000", "y2010", sparse = True)
graphoskop.earth_movers_distance(net, a, b, component=0, weight=None, inverse=False, normalize=True, flow=False)

Earth mover’s distance between two node vectors: the least total cost of moving the mass of a (the amount on every node) so that it becomes b, moving a unit of mass from node \(i\) to node \(j\) costing the length of the shortest path between them (Wasserstein-1 distance with the shortest path metric).

On a network, the optimal transport moves mass along edges, so the distance is the minimum cost flow \(\min_f \sum_e l_e f_e\) subject to \(f \geq 0\) and, for every node, inflow minus outflow \(= b_i - a_i\) (Beckmann’s formulation): a sparse linear program with one variable per edge, instead of one per pair of nodes, solved exactly with HiGHS (on the CPU). On directed networks mass moves only along the direction of the edges, and the distance is infinite if some mass cannot reach where it is needed; undirected edges carry mass both ways. Self loops are ignored. In multilayer networks, mass moves between copies through couplings, which cost their length like any edge.

The solver takes about a second on 10,000 nodes and 30,000 edges, and one to two minutes on 100,000 nodes and 300,000 edges (one call, without a progress bar).

normalize makes the two masses equal: True rescales both vectors to sum to 1 (probability distributions: the distance is then an average path length); "min" rescales the vector with the larger sum to the smaller sum, and "max" the one with the smaller sum to the larger sum (keeping the units of the vectors: the distance is in units of mass times length); with False, the sums must already be equal.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • a – Node attribute (position in net.node_attributes or name), or vector of length n (numpy.ndarray or torch.Tensor), with non negative entries.

  • b – Second node vector, same modalities as a.

  • component – Index of the component (0 is the largest), or None for all components. With None, vectors are given as lists with one entry per component (or as attributes).

  • weight – Name of the edge attribute used as edge length, or None for unit lengths.

  • inverse – Use the reciprocal of weight as length (for weights measuring proximity, a zero weight then being no edge).

  • normalize – True (both to sum 1), "min" (both to the smaller sum), "max" (both to the larger sum) or False (no rescaling).

  • flow – Whether to return the optimal transport too.

Returns:

Distance (inf if impossible, NaN if a vector has missing values); with flow, (distance, flows): the mass moved along every edge of the component, from its source to its target, as a vector aligned with the edges (store it with set_edge_attribute()). A list of them if component is None.

Raises:

ValueError – If a vector has negative entries, sums to zero with normalize, the sums differ without it, normalize is not valid, or some lengths are negative.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", "nodes.tsv")
d = gsk.earth_movers_distance(net, "supply", "demand")
d, f = gsk.earth_movers_distance(net, "supply", "demand", weight = "km", flow = True)
d = gsk.earth_movers_distance(net, "supply", "demand", normalize = "max")   # demand to the supply total
net.set_edge_attribute("shipped", f)
graphoskop.generalized_euclidean(net, a, b=None, component=0, lpinv=None, sparse=False, weight=None, kind='out', q=0.25, alpha=0.15)

Generalized Euclidean distance \(\sqrt{(a - b)^H L^+ (a - b)}\) between two node vectors, or norm \(\sqrt{a^H L^+ a}\) of a single one, with \(L^+\) the pseudoinverse of the Laplacian (see laplacian() for the available Laplacians).

\(L^+\) is used as given in lpinv, either as a dense tensor (from laplacian_pinv()) or as a solver (from laplacian_solver()). If lpinv is None it is computed, densely or, if sparse, as a solver. Pass lpinv to reuse it across calls.

For Hermitian Laplacians (undirected, "magnetic", "chung") the quadratic form is non negative. The "out" Laplacian is not symmetric, so the quadratic form can be negative, and the result is then NaN. Missing attribute values propagate as NaN.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • a – Node attribute (position in net.node_attributes or name), or vector of length n (numpy.ndarray or torch.Tensor).

  • b – Second node vector, same modalities as a, or None.

  • component – Index of the component (0 is the largest), or None for all components. With None, vectors and lpinv are given as lists with one entry per component.

  • lpinv – Cached pseudoinverse or solver, or None.

  • sparse – Compute a solver instead of the dense pseudoinverse when lpinv is None.

  • weight – Name of the edge attribute used as edge weight, or None for unit weights.

  • kind – Laplacian of directed networks: "out", "magnetic" or "chung".

  • q – Charge of the magnetic Laplacian.

  • alpha – Teleportation probability of the Chung Laplacian.

Returns:

Distance, or list of distances if component is None.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", "nodes.tsv")
d = gsk.generalized_euclidean(net, "age", "height")       # attributes by name
d = gsk.generalized_euclidean(net, 0, sparse = True)      # norm, attribute by position
lp = gsk.laplacian_pinv(net)
d = gsk.generalized_euclidean(net, x, y, lpinv = lp)      # vectors, cached pseudoinverse
ds = gsk.generalized_euclidean(net, "age", component = None)
graphoskop.graph_fourier_basis(net, component=0, k=None, normalized=False, weight=None, kind='out', q=0.25, alpha=0.15)

Graph Fourier basis: the eigenvalues (frequencies) and eigenvectors (Fourier modes) of the Laplacian, \(L = U \Lambda U^H\), in increasing order of frequency (Shuman, Narang, Frossard, Ortega and Vandergheynst, 2013). Low frequencies vary little across edges (the first one, 0, is constant on a connected undirected network), high frequencies change sign between neighbors. Used by graph_fourier_transform() and inverse_graph_fourier_transform(); compute it once and pass it as their basis to transform many signals.

The Laplacian must be Hermitian: undirected, or kind = "magnetic" or "chung" on directed networks (see laplacian(); the magnetic one has complex modes). With normalized, the Laplacian is \(D^{-1 / 2} L D^{-1 / 2}\) (with \(D\) its diagonal), e.g. \(I - D^{-1 / 2} A D^{-1 / 2}\) on undirected networks, whose frequencies are between 0 and 2.

With k = None, all n modes, from a dense eigendecomposition on the device of the network (\(O(n^3)\) time, \(n^2\) memory: up to some tens of thousands of nodes on a GPU). With k, only the k lowest frequencies: dense if n is up to 2,000 or k more than a quarter of n, otherwise sparse (Lanczos, on the CPU, with n k memory), for large networks. The phase of every mode is fixed (its largest entry, in modulus, is real and positive), so the basis is reproducible; modes of repeated frequencies are any orthonormal basis of their eigenspace. Computed in double precision.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • component – Index of the component (0 is the largest), or None for all components (returns a list).

  • k – Number of modes (lowest frequencies), or None for all.

  • normalized – Whether to use the normalized Laplacian.

  • weight – Name of the edge attribute used as edge weight, or None for unit weights.

  • kind – Laplacian of directed networks: "magnetic" or "chung" ("out" is not Hermitian).

  • q – Charge of the magnetic Laplacian.

  • alpha – Teleportation probability of the Chung Laplacian.

Returns:

(frequencies, modes): vector of length k and n by k matrix (one mode per column), or a list of them if component is None.

Raises:

ValueError – If the Laplacian is not Hermitian or k is not between 1 and n.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", "nodes.tsv")
freq, U = gsk.graph_fourier_basis(net)                     # all modes
freq, U = gsk.graph_fourier_basis(net, k = 100, normalized = True)
graphoskop.graph_fourier_transform(net, a, component=0, basis=None, k=None, normalized=False, weight=None, kind='out', q=0.25, alpha=0.15)

Graph Fourier transform of a signal on the nodes, \(\hat{a} = U^H a\): its coefficients on the Fourier modes of graph_fourier_basis(), from the lowest frequency to the highest. A signal that varies little across edges (smooth) has most of its energy in the first coefficients; \(\sum_l \lambda_l |\hat{a}_l|^2 = a^H L a\) is its total variation. With all n modes the transform is invertible (inverse_graph_fourier_transform()) and keeps the norm (Parseval); with k modes, it gives the k lowest frequencies only.

basis (from graph_fourier_basis()) is used as given; if None, it is computed with k, normalized and the Laplacian arguments (see there). Pass it to transform many signals. Missing values (NaN) make all coefficients of their signal NaN.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • a – Node attribute (position in net.node_attributes or name), list of node attributes, vector of length n or n by s matrix of s signals (numpy.ndarray or torch.Tensor).

  • component – Index of the component (0 is the largest), or None for all components. With None, vectors and basis are given as lists with one entry per component.

  • basis – (frequencies, modes) from graph_fourier_basis(), or None.

  • k – Number of modes, if basis is None (None for all).

  • normalized – Whether to use the normalized Laplacian, if basis is None.

  • weight – Name of the edge attribute used as edge weight, or None for unit weights.

  • kind – Laplacian of directed networks: "magnetic" or "chung".

  • q – Charge of the magnetic Laplacian.

  • alpha – Teleportation probability of the Chung Laplacian.

Returns:

Coefficients: vector of length k (or k by s matrix), in double precision (complex for the magnetic Laplacian), on the device of the network; or a list of them if component is None. The frequencies are basis[0].

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", "nodes.tsv")
freq, U = basis = gsk.graph_fourier_basis(net)
c = gsk.graph_fourier_transform(net, "income", basis = basis)
C = gsk.graph_fourier_transform(net, ["income", "age"], basis = basis)   # one column each
low = (c[:10] ** 2).sum() / (c ** 2).sum()                   # energy in the 10 lowest frequencies
graphoskop.inverse_graph_fourier_transform(net, coefficients, component=0, basis=None, k=None, normalized=False, weight=None, kind='out', q=0.25, alpha=0.15)

Inverse graph Fourier transform, \(a = U \hat{a}\): the signal on the nodes with the given coefficients on the Fourier modes of graph_fourier_basis(). With all n modes, it recovers the signal of graph_fourier_transform(); with k modes, its projection on the k lowest frequencies (a low pass filter). Changing the coefficients in between filters the signal, e.g. h(freq) * c for a filter h.

basis is used as given, or computed as in graph_fourier_transform(); its number of modes must match the number of coefficients.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • coefficients – Vector of k coefficients, or k by s matrix of s signals (numpy.ndarray or torch.Tensor).

  • component – Index of the component (0 is the largest), or None for all components. With None, coefficients and basis are given as lists with one entry per component.

  • basis – (frequencies, modes) from graph_fourier_basis(), or None.

  • k – Number of modes, if basis is None (None for all).

  • normalized – Whether to use the normalized Laplacian, if basis is None.

  • weight – Name of the edge attribute used as edge weight, or None for unit weights.

  • kind – Laplacian of directed networks: "magnetic" or "chung".

  • q – Charge of the magnetic Laplacian.

  • alpha – Teleportation probability of the Chung Laplacian.

Returns:

Node vector of length n (or n by s matrix) in double precision, real unless the modes are complex, on the device of the network; or a list of them if component is None. Store it with set_node_attribute().

Raises:

ValueError – If the number of coefficients is not the number of modes.

Example:

import torch
import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", "nodes.tsv")
freq, U = basis = gsk.graph_fourier_basis(net)
c = gsk.graph_fourier_transform(net, "income", basis = basis)
smooth = gsk.inverse_graph_fourier_transform(net, c * (freq < 0.5), basis = basis)
heat = gsk.inverse_graph_fourier_transform(net, c * torch.exp(-2 * freq), basis = basis)
graphoskop.network_correlation(net, a, b, component=0, W=None, k=1, lpinv=None, weight=None, kind='out', q=0.25, alpha=0.15)

Network Pearson correlation (Coscia, 2021; Coscia and Devriendt, 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09489):

\[\rho_{a, b, G} = \frac{\hat{a}^T W \hat{b}}{\sqrt{(\hat{a}^T W \hat{a}) (\hat{b}^T W \hat{b})}},\]

with \(\hat{a} = a - \bar{a}\) the centered vectors. The numerator is \(\sum W \times (\hat{a} \otimes \hat{b})\): every pair of entries is compared, weighted by the proximity of the two nodes. With \(W = I\) this is the Pearson correlation.

The default weights are \(W = e^{-k \Omega}\) (entrywise), with \(\Omega\) the effective resistance matrix (see effective_resistance()). The correlation is real and in \([-1, 1]\) when \(W\) is positive definite on vectors with zero sum, which holds for any \(k > 0\) when \(\Omega\) is a metric of negative type: true for undirected, "chung" and "magnetic" Laplacians, not guaranteed for "out". k sets how fast proximity decays: as \(k \to 0\) all pairs weigh the same, as \(k \to \infty\) the result tends to the Pearson correlation. Choose it relative to the scale of \(\Omega\): the "chung" Laplacian is scaled by the stationary distribution, which sums to 1, so its resistances are about n times larger and need a k about n times smaller.

\(W\) can be passed precomputed (an n by n tensor), to reuse it across calls; it is then used as given, and k, lpinv and the Laplacian arguments are ignored. Otherwise it is computed from lpinv (cached dense pseudoinverse) or from scratch, in place, needing one n by n matrix besides lpinv.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • a – Node attribute (position in net.node_attributes or name), or vector of length n (numpy.ndarray or torch.Tensor).

  • b – Second node vector, same modalities as a.

  • component – Index of the component (0 is the largest), or None for all components. With None, vectors, W and lpinv are given as lists with one entry per component.

  • W – Precomputed weight matrix, or None.

  • k – Scale of the effective resistance in \(W = e^{-k \Omega}\).

  • lpinv – Cached dense pseudoinverse of the Laplacian, or None.

  • weight – Name of the edge attribute used as edge weight, or None for unit weights.

  • kind – Laplacian of directed networks: "out", "magnetic" or "chung".

  • q – Charge of the magnetic Laplacian.

  • alpha – Teleportation probability of the Chung Laplacian.

Returns:

Correlation, or list of correlations if component is None. NaN for constant vectors or missing values.

Example:

import torch
import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", "nodes.tsv")
r = gsk.network_correlation(net, "age", "height")
W = torch.exp(-2 * gsk.effective_resistance(net))         # cached weights, k = 2
r = gsk.network_correlation(net, "age", "height", W = W)
graphoskop.network_entropy(net, a, component=0, W=None, k=1, lpinv=None, weight=None, kind='out', q=0.25, alpha=0.15)

Network entropy of a node vector interpreted as a probability distribution:

\[H(p) = -\sum_i p_i \log (W p)_i,\]

with \(p = a / \sum_i a_i\) and node similarities \(W = e^{-k \Omega}\) (entrywise), \(\Omega\) being the effective resistance matrix (see effective_resistance()). \((W p)_i\) is the probability mass near node \(i\), so mass spread over distant nodes gives a high entropy and mass concentrated on nearby nodes a low one. With \(W = I\) this is the Shannon entropy. It is the order 1 similarity-sensitive diversity of Leinster and Cobbold (2012). Nodes with \(p_i = 0\) add nothing to the sum.

a must be a probability mass up to normalization: a ValueError is raised if it has negative entries or sums to zero.

\(W\) can be passed precomputed (an n by n tensor), to reuse it across calls (for instance the one of network_correlation()); it is then used as given, and k, lpinv and the Laplacian arguments are ignored. Otherwise it is computed from lpinv (cached dense pseudoinverse) or from scratch. As for network_correlation(), choose k relative to the scale of \(\Omega\).

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • a – Node attribute (position in net.node_attributes or name), or vector of length n (numpy.ndarray or torch.Tensor), with non negative entries.

  • component – Index of the component (0 is the largest), or None for all components. With None, vectors, W and lpinv are given as lists with one entry per component.

  • W – Precomputed similarity matrix, or None for \(e^{-k \Omega}\).

  • k – Scale of the effective resistance in \(W = e^{-k \Omega}\).

  • lpinv – Cached dense pseudoinverse of the Laplacian, or None.

  • weight – Name of the edge attribute used as edge weight, or None for unit weights.

  • kind – Laplacian of directed networks: "out", "magnetic" or "chung".

  • q – Charge of the magnetic Laplacian.

  • alpha – Teleportation probability of the Chung Laplacian.

Returns:

Entropy (in nats), or list of entropies if component is None. NaN for missing values.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", "nodes.tsv")
h = gsk.network_entropy(net, "population")
graphoskop.network_variance(net, a, component=0, R=None, lpinv=None, weight=None, kind='out', q=0.25, alpha=0.15)

Network variance of a node vector interpreted as a probability distribution (Devriendt, Martin-Gutierrez and Lambiotte, 2022, https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.09155):

\[\mathrm{var}(p) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i, j} p_i p_j d^2(i, j) = \frac{1}{2} p^T \Omega p,\]

with \(p = a / \sum_i a_i\), \(\Omega\) the effective resistance matrix (see effective_resistance()) and \(d = \sqrt{\Omega}\) the square root effective resistance distance, as in the paper. On an undirected network, Kemeny’s constant is \(\kappa = 2 m \, \mathrm{var}(\pi)\), with \(\pi\) the stationary distribution of the random walk and \(m\) the total edge weight, and the Kirchhoff index is \(n^2 \, \mathrm{var}(u / n)\), with \(u / n\) the uniform distribution.

a must be a probability mass up to normalization: a ValueError is raised if it has negative entries or sums to zero.

\(\Omega\) can be passed precomputed as R, to reuse it across calls; it is then used as given and left unchanged, and lpinv and the Laplacian arguments are ignored. Otherwise it is computed from lpinv (cached dense pseudoinverse) or from scratch.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • a – Node attribute (position in net.node_attributes or name), or vector of length n (numpy.ndarray or torch.Tensor), with non negative entries.

  • component – Index of the component (0 is the largest), or None for all components. With None, vectors, R and lpinv are given as lists with one entry per component.

  • R – Precomputed effective resistance matrix, or None.

  • lpinv – Cached dense pseudoinverse of the Laplacian, or None.

  • weight – Name of the edge attribute used as edge weight, or None for unit weights.

  • kind – Laplacian of directed networks: "out", "magnetic" or "chung".

  • q – Charge of the magnetic Laplacian.

  • alpha – Teleportation probability of the Chung Laplacian.

Returns:

Variance, or list of variances if component is None. NaN for missing values.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", "nodes.tsv")
v = gsk.network_variance(net, "population")
R = gsk.effective_resistance(net)
v = gsk.network_variance(net, "income", R = R)       # cached resistances
graphoskop.pairwise_generalized_euclidean(net, component=0, lpinv=None, sparse=None, weight=None, kind='out', q=0.25, alpha=0.15, output_device=None, output_dtype=torch.float64)

Generalized Euclidean distances between all pairs of node attributes.

Works like generalized_euclidean(), applied to every pair of columns of node_attr. Instead of one product with \(L^+\) per pair, it computes \(Y = L^+ X\) once for the k attributes \(X\) and then all squared distances from the k by k matrix \(G = X^H Y\), as \(G_{ii} + G_{jj} - G_{ij} - G_{ji}\). The attributes are first centered across attributes for each node, which leaves the distances unchanged and reduces rounding errors. The cost is k products with \(L^+\) (or k solves, run together) plus an n by k by k product.

\(L^+\) is used as given in lpinv (dense tensor or solver). If lpinv is None, a solver is built when sparse is True or None (the default, usually faster, since only k solves are needed), and the dense pseudoinverse when sparse is False.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • component – Index of the component (0 is the largest), or None for all components. With None, lpinv is given as a list with one entry per component.

  • lpinv – Cached pseudoinverse or solver, or None.

  • sparse – Laplacian solver (True or None) or dense pseudoinverse (False) when lpinv is None.

  • weight – Name of the edge attribute used as edge weight, or None for unit weights.

  • kind – Laplacian of directed networks: "out", "magnetic" or "chung".

  • q – Charge of the magnetic Laplacian.

  • alpha – Teleportation probability of the Chung Laplacian.

  • output_device – Device of the result, None for the device of the component. With "cpu" the computation still runs on the device of the component (e.g. the GPU), one block of rows at a time, and only the result is kept on the CPU: useful when the k by k result does not fit in GPU memory.

  • output_dtype – Dtype of the result. torch.float32 halves its memory (a k by k float64 matrix needs \(8 k^2\) bytes, 3.2 GB for 20,000 attributes); the computation itself stays in double precision.

Returns:

k by k tensor, rows and columns ordered as net.node_attributes, or a list of them if component is None. NaN where the quadratic form is negative (possible only for the "out" Laplacian) or for attributes with missing values.

Network Pearson correlations between all pairs of node attributes.

Works like network_correlation(), applied to every pair of columns of node_attr. With \(\hat{X}\) the centered attributes, it computes \(G = \hat{X}^T W \hat{X}\) with a single product of \(W\) with the n by k matrix \(\hat{X}\), and then all correlations as \(G_{ij} / \sqrt{G_{ii} G_{jj}}\). \(W\) is computed once, or reused if given.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • component – Index of the component (0 is the largest), or None for all components. With None, W and lpinv are given as lists with one entry per component.

  • W – Precomputed weight matrix, or None for \(e^{-k \Omega}\).

  • k – Scale of the effective resistance in \(W = e^{-k \Omega}\).

  • lpinv – Cached dense pseudoinverse of the Laplacian, or None.

  • weight – Name of the edge attribute used as edge weight, or None for unit weights.

  • kind – Laplacian of directed networks: "out", "magnetic" or "chung".

  • q – Charge of the magnetic Laplacian.

  • alpha – Teleportation probability of the Chung Laplacian.

  • output_device – Device of the result, None for the device of the component. With "cpu" the computation still runs on the device of the component (e.g. the GPU), one block of rows at a time, and only the result is kept on the CPU: useful when the k by k result does not fit in GPU memory.

  • output_dtype – Dtype of the result. torch.float32 halves its memory (a k by k float64 matrix needs \(8 k^2\) bytes, 3.2 GB for 20,000 attributes); the computation itself stays in double precision.

Returns:

k by k tensor, rows and columns ordered as net.node_attributes, or a list of them if component is None. NaN for constant attributes or missing values.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", "nodes.tsv")
C = gsk.pairwise_network_correlation(net, weight = "weight")
