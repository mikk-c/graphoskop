Generators

Synthetic network generators, in seven families:

  • simple graphs, with simple fixed topologies: chain_graph();

  • random graphs, wired at random given a few numbers (edges, density, the degree of every node, a small initiator matrix, or positions in space): gnm_random_graph(), gnp_random_graph(), configuration_model(), edge_swap() (only randomizes a network), kronecker_graph(), geometric_graph();

  • clustered graphs, with many triangles: caveman_graph(), watts_strogatz_graph();

  • broad degree graphs, with hubs: barabasi_albert_graph(), link_selection_graph(), copying_graph(), holme_kim_graph() (with clustering);

  • stochastic blockmodels, with communities: planted_partition_graph(), stochastic_block_model(), degree_corrected_block_model(), mixed_membership_block_model() (overlapping communities), nested_block_model() (hierarchy of groups);

  • community benchmarks, with planted communities to test community discovery: girvan_newman_graph(), lfr_benchmark_graph() (broad degrees and community sizes; directed, weighted, overlapping);

  • higher-order networks (see Higher-order networks): simplicial complexes with growing_manifold() (discrete manifolds grown simplex by simplex) and simplicial_configuration_model() (given facets per node and facet dimensions), hypergraphs with hypergraph_configuration_model() (given hyperedges per node and hyperedge sizes).

Every random generator takes its natural parameters (e.g. the number of nodes and edges) or, with net, an existing network, from which it infers them: the result is then a randomization of net. A randomization keeps the nodes of net, with their ids, numeric and qualitative attributes, node types (bipartite networks) and layers (multilayer networks), and the device; only the edges are generated anew. Parameters given explicitly together with net override the inferred ones, when they can change without changing the nodes.

New edges take their attributes from the edges they replace: each gets the attribute row of one original edge, drawn at random without replacement; if there are more new edges than original ones, every original row is used once and the extra edges get rows drawn with replacement. Weights and signs (see Signed networks) are therefore shuffled over the new edges, keeping their distribution.

All random numbers are drawn with torch on the device of the network, so results depend on seed and on the device.

randomization_test() compares a statistic of a network with its distribution over the randomizations of any of these generators (or networks drawn from a fitted ERGM).

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", "nodes.tsv")                # column weight; age, city
rnd = gsk.gnm_random_graph(net = net, seed = 1)                 # same nodes, attributes and m
rnd.get_edge_attribute("weight")                                # the weights of net, shuffled

ml = gsk.load_network("multiplex.tsv", multilayer = True, coupling = "all")
rnd = gsk.gnm_random_graph(net = ml, m = {"work": 100})         # each layer on its own; couplings kept

Broad degree graphs

Growing networks whose new nodes link more to nodes with more links, so that a few hubs emerge and degrees follow a power law: by knowing all degrees (preferential attachment), or locally, through random links (link selection) or the links of random nodes (copying); with triangles closed by new nodes, degrees stay broad and clustering stays high (Holme-Kim).

ba = gsk.barabasi_albert_graph(10000, 6, seed = 1)              # average degree 6: 3 edges per new node
ls = gsk.link_selection_graph(10000, 6, seed = 1)               # each new node picks 3 random links
cp = gsk.copying_graph(10000, 6, seed = 1)                      # each new node copies 3 links of random nodes
hk = gsk.holme_kim_graph(10000, 6, 0.8, seed = 1)               # power law with clustering
rnd = gsk.holme_kim_graph(net = net)                            # n, m and average clustering of net
gsk.degree_power_law(ls)                                        # exponent about 3
rnd = gsk.barabasi_albert_graph(net = net)                      # n and m of net
graphoskop.barabasi_albert_graph(n=None, k=None, net=None, seed=None, device=None)

Barabási-Albert graph (Barabási and Albert, 1999): nodes arrive one at a time and link to earlier nodes chosen with probability proportional to their degree (preferential attachment), giving a power law degree distribution with exponent about 3.

k is the average degree: each node brings k / 2 edges, to distinct earlier nodes, so k = 2 m is the classic model with m edges per node. The graph starts from the first k / 2 + 1 nodes, each linked to all the earlier ones (a clique). Any k is allowed: for odd (or fractional) k, evenly spaced nodes bring one edge more, so that the graph has exactly round(n k / 2) edges. Probabilities are those before the node arrives, as in the original model and in NetworkX (whose seed is a star instead of a clique). The graph is undirected, without self loops nor parallel edges, and connected if k is at least 2.

Arrivals are simulated in parallel on the device: each new edge copies an endpoint at a random position in the list of the earlier edges (Batagelj and Brandes, 2005), copies of copies are resolved together, and targets a node already chose are drawn again.

Without net, nodes are the integers 0 to n - 1 in order of arrival. With net, the result is a randomization of net (see the module description): its nodes arrive in random order, and n and the number of edges (so k) are those of net, unless k is given. In multilayer networks each layer is randomized on its own (k can be one value for all layers or a dictionary from layer to average degree); interlayer edges are kept.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: not supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • n – Number of nodes; None with net.

  • k – Average degree; None with net, for its own.

  • net – An undirected, not bipartite Network to randomize, or None.

  • seed – Seed of the random numbers, or None for a random seed.

  • device – Device of the result, e.g. "cuda"; None for the CPU, or for the device of net.

Returns:

The Barabási-Albert graph, a new Network.

Raises:

ValueError – If neither n and k nor net are given, if net is directed or bipartite, if n or device differ from those of net, if there are more edges than node pairs, or for dictionaries as in gnm_random_graph().

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

g = gsk.barabasi_albert_graph(10000, 6, seed = 1)          # m = 3 edges per new node
gsk.degree_power_law(g)                                    # exponent about 3

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", "nodes.tsv")
rnd = gsk.barabasi_albert_graph(net = net, seed = 1)       # same n and m, nodes arrive at random
graphoskop.copying_graph(n=None, k=None, net=None, seed=None, device=None)

Copying graph: nodes arrive one at a time, and each picks an earlier node uniformly at random and copies one of its connections, chosen uniformly at random: it links to a random neighbor of a random node.

Like link_selection_graph(), this is a local process: the new node knows nothing about the network, and picks a node instead of a link. A node is reached through each of its neighbors \(u\), with probability \(1 / (N k_u)\) for \(N\) earlier nodes, so the more neighbors a node has, the more likely it is to be copied. Since the neighbors of hubs tend to have few neighbors themselves, hubs are copied even more than by preferential attachment: degrees are broader than in barabasi_albert_graph(), with a power law exponent close to 2 instead of 3 (as for pure copying in Kumar et al., 2000).

As in barabasi_albert_graph(), k is the average degree (each node makes k / 2 copies, of distinct neighbors, so k = 2 m makes m copies), any k gives exactly round(n k / 2) edges, the first k / 2 + 1 nodes form a clique, and a copy is drawn again (another random node, another random neighbor) if it gives a node already chosen. Nodes and neighborhoods are those before the node arrives. The graph is undirected, without self loops nor parallel edges, and connected if k is at least 2.

Each copy depends on the whole neighborhood of a node, so unlike the other generators arrivals are simulated one after the other, in Python on the CPU (about 12 seconds per million nodes with k = 10), and the result is then moved to device.

Without net, nodes are the integers 0 to n - 1 in order of arrival. With net, the result is a randomization of net (see the module description): its nodes arrive in random order, and n and the number of edges (so k) are those of net, unless k is given. In multilayer networks each layer is randomized on its own (k can be one value for all layers or a dictionary from layer to average degree); interlayer edges are kept.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: not supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • n – Number of nodes; None with net.

  • k – Average degree; None with net, for its own.

  • net – An undirected, not bipartite Network to randomize, or None.

  • seed – Seed of the random numbers, or None for a random seed.

  • device – Device of the result, e.g. "cuda"; None for the CPU, or for the device of net.

Returns:

The copying graph, a new Network.

Raises:

ValueError – If neither n and k nor net are given, if net is directed or bipartite, if n or device differ from those of net, if there are more edges than node pairs, or for dictionaries as in gnm_random_graph().

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

g = gsk.copying_graph(10000, 6, seed = 1)                   # each new node makes 3 copies
gsk.degree_power_law(g)

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", "nodes.tsv")
rnd = gsk.copying_graph(net = net, seed = 1)                # same n and m, nodes arrive at random
graphoskop.holme_kim_graph(n=None, k=None, p=None, net=None, seed=None, device=None)

Holme-Kim graph (Holme and Kim, 2002): a power law with clustering. Nodes arrive one at a time as in barabasi_albert_graph(), but after linking to a node by preferential attachment, each further edge of the new node is, with probability p, a triad formation step: it links to a random neighbor of the node just chosen by preferential attachment, closing a triangle.

Degrees follow a power law with exponent about 3, as in preferential attachment, while p tunes the clustering, from that of barabasi_albert_graph() (p = 0) to a high value that does not vanish as the graph grows (p = 1).

As in barabasi_albert_graph(), k is the average degree (each node brings k / 2 edges, to distinct nodes, so k = 2 m is the model with m edges per node), any k gives exactly round(n k / 2) edges, the first k / 2 + 1 nodes form a clique, a node already chosen is drawn again, and degrees and neighbors are those before the node arrives. The first edge of a node is always by preferential attachment, and a triad formation step whose node has no neighbor left falls back to it. The graph is undirected, without self loops nor parallel edges, and connected if k is at least 2.

Triad formation needs the neighbors of a node when the new node arrives, so, as for copying_graph(), arrivals are simulated one after the other, in Python on the CPU, and the result is then moved to device.

Without net, nodes are the integers 0 to n - 1 in order of arrival. With net, the result is a randomization of net (see the module description): its nodes arrive in random order, and n and the number of edges (so k) are those of net, unless k is given. p too is inferred, unless given: it is the probability whose graphs have the average clustering (average_clustering()) of net, found by simulation (regula falsi on p, on graphs of at most 100,000 nodes with the average degree of the result, all with the same random numbers; clustering varies little with size beyond a few thousand nodes), which costs a few extra generations. A network more clustered than any Holme-Kim graph of its density gets p = 1, with a warning. In multilayer networks each layer is randomized on its own, with its own p (k and p can be one value for all layers or a dictionary from layer to value); interlayer edges are kept.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: not supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • n – Number of nodes; None with net.

  • k – Average degree; None with net, for its own.

  • p – Probability of a triad formation step, after the first edge of each node; None with net, to match its average clustering.

  • net – An undirected, not bipartite Network to randomize, or None.

  • seed – Seed of the random numbers, or None for a random seed.

  • device – Device of the result, e.g. "cuda"; None for the CPU, or for the device of net.

Returns:

The Holme-Kim graph, a new Network.

Raises:

ValueError – If p is not between 0 and 1, or missing without net, if neither n and k nor net are given, if net is directed or bipartite, if n or device differ from those of net, if there are more edges than node pairs, or for dictionaries as in gnm_random_graph().

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

g = gsk.holme_kim_graph(10000, 6, 0.8, seed = 1)            # 3 edges per node, mostly closing triangles
gsk.average_clustering(g), gsk.degree_power_law(g)

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", "nodes.tsv")
rnd = gsk.holme_kim_graph(net = net, seed = 1)              # same n, m and average clustering
rnd = gsk.holme_kim_graph(net = net, p = 0.5, seed = 1)     # same n and m, p given
graphoskop.link_selection_graph(n=None, k=None, net=None, seed=None, device=None)

Link selection graph: nodes arrive one at a time, and each picks random links of the network and connects to one of the two nodes of each, chosen uniformly at random.

This is a local process: a node needs to know a few random links, not everyone’s degree. Yet a node with degree \(k_i\) is an endpoint of \(k_i\) of the \(2 m\) endpoint slots, so it is reached with probability \(k_i / 2 m\): exactly linear preferential attachment, and the same broad degree distribution as barabasi_albert_graph() (power law with exponent about 3). The only difference is that a node never uses the same link twice: once linked to a node through a link, it cannot reach the other node of that link through it.

As in barabasi_albert_graph(), k is the average degree (each node brings k / 2 links, to distinct nodes, so k = 2 m picks m links), any k gives exactly round(n k / 2) edges, the first k / 2 + 1 nodes form a clique, a link or a node already used is drawn again, and links are picked among those existing before the node arrives. The graph is undirected, without self loops nor parallel edges, connected if k is at least 2, and generated in parallel on the device, with the same result as the sequential process.

Without net, nodes are the integers 0 to n - 1 in order of arrival. With net, the result is a randomization of net (see the module description): its nodes arrive in random order, and n and the number of edges (so k) are those of net, unless k is given. In multilayer networks each layer is randomized on its own (k can be one value for all layers or a dictionary from layer to average degree); interlayer edges are kept.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: not supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • n – Number of nodes; None with net.

  • k – Average degree; None with net, for its own.

  • net – An undirected, not bipartite Network to randomize, or None.

  • seed – Seed of the random numbers, or None for a random seed.

  • device – Device of the result, e.g. "cuda"; None for the CPU, or for the device of net.

Returns:

The link selection graph, a new Network.

Raises:

ValueError – If neither n and k nor net are given, if net is directed or bipartite, if n or device differ from those of net, if there are more edges than node pairs, or for dictionaries as in gnm_random_graph().

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

g = gsk.link_selection_graph(10000, 6, seed = 1)            # each new node picks 3 links
gsk.degree_power_law(g)                                     # exponent about 3, as preferential attachment

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", "nodes.tsv")
rnd = gsk.link_selection_graph(net = net, seed = 1)         # same n and m, nodes arrive at random

Clustered graphs

Many triangles: cliques (caves), or neighborhoods on a ring with a few random shortcuts (small world).

c = gsk.caveman_graph(10, 5, connected = True)                  # 10 cliques of 5 nodes in a ring
gsk.modularity(c, "cave")                                       # planted partition, node attribute
rnd = gsk.caveman_graph(net = net, seed = 1)                    # nodes of net in random caves

w = gsk.watts_strogatz_graph(1000, 6, 0.1, connected = True)    # small world, one component
w = gsk.watts_strogatz_graph(1000, 6, 0.1, rewire = "add")     # Newman-Watts shortcuts
rnd = gsk.watts_strogatz_graph(net = net, p = 0.1)              # n and m of net, p always given
graphoskop.caveman_graph(l=None, k=None, net=None, connected=False, directed=None, attribute='cave', seed=None, device=None)

Caveman graph (Watts, 1999): l caves, each a clique of k nodes.

Caves are disconnected, or, with connected = True, joined in a ring (the connected caveman graph): in every cave the edge between its first two nodes is removed, and its first node is linked to the last node of the previous cave instead, so that every node keeps degree k - 1 (a cave of one node only gains its ring edge). Directed caveman graphs have every edge in both directions. The cave of every node is stored as the numeric node attribute attribute, a planted partition for e.g. modularity().

Without net, nodes are the integers 0 to n - 1, cave by cave; k can also be a list of cave sizes, for caves of different sizes. With net, the result is a randomization of net (see the module description): its nodes are assigned at random to caves, whose sizes differ by at most one. As every node of a caveman graph has degree k - 1, the size of the caves follows from the average degree of net: the number of caves is the one that gives the number of edges closest to that of net (pairs of nodes in either direction, if directed), unless l (number of caves) or k (cave size, from which l is n / k rounded) is given. In multilayer networks each layer is randomized on its own, with its own caves (numbered across layers, so that no two layers share one); l can be a dictionary from layer to number of caves, k one size for all layers or a dictionary. Interlayer edges are kept. The new edges take the attributes of the old ones of their layer.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: not supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • l – Number of caves; None with net, or if k is a list.

  • k – Number of nodes of each cave, or list of cave sizes; None with net.

  • net – A Network to randomize, or None.

  • connected – Whether to join the caves in a ring.

  • directed – Whether the edges are directed (both directions); None for undirected, or for the direction of net.

  • attribute – Name of the node attribute holding the cave of each node (overwritten if net has it), or None for no attribute.

  • seed – Seed of the random numbers (with net), or None for a random seed.

  • device – Device of the result, e.g. "cuda"; None for the CPU, or for the device of net.

Returns:

The caveman graph, a new Network.

Raises:

ValueError – If neither l and k nor net are given, if both l and k are given with net, if net is bipartite (it has no cliques), if directed or device differ from those of net, if a layer cannot be split into l caves, or for dictionaries as in gnm_random_graph().

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

g = gsk.caveman_graph(10, 5)                                   # 10 disconnected 5-cliques
g = gsk.caveman_graph(10, 5, connected = True)                 # in a ring: one component
g = gsk.caveman_graph(k = [3, 4, 5])                           # caves of different sizes
gsk.modularity(g, "cave")

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", "nodes.tsv")
rnd = gsk.caveman_graph(net = net, seed = 1)                   # nodes in random caves, same average degree
gsk.assortativity(rnd, "age")                                  # attributes of net, on caves
graphoskop.watts_strogatz_graph(n=None, k=None, p=None, net=None, rewire='target', connected=False, tries=100, seed=None, device=None)

Watts-Strogatz small world graph (Watts and Strogatz, 1998): a ring lattice whose edges are rewired at random with probability p.

The n nodes sit on a ring, each linked to its nearest neighbors: with average degree k, the lattice has round(n k / 2) edges, linking every node to the k / 2 nearest nodes on each side for even k (the lattice of NetworkX). Any k is allowed: for odd (or fractional) k the last distance links only some pairs, evenly spaced, so that the lattice has exactly the number of edges asked and degrees differ by at most one whenever possible. Then each edge, independently with probability p, is rewired according to rewire:

  • "target": one endpoint moves, as in the original model: (u, v) becomes (u, w), w uniformly random among the nodes not linked to u. All picked edges are rewired at once (on the device) instead of one after the other; in very dense graphs, an edge whose node is already linked to all the others moves to a random free pair instead;

  • "both": both endpoints move: the edge becomes a uniformly random pair of unlinked nodes;

  • "add": the edge stays, and a new edge joins a uniformly random pair of unlinked nodes (Newman and Watts, 1999), so there are about (1 + p) n k / 2 edges (at most all pairs) and the lattice is never broken.

The graph has no self loops nor parallel edges, and is undirected. With connected = True, rewiring is drawn again, up to tries times, until the graph is connected (the lattice must be, so k at least 2).

Without net, nodes are the integers 0 to n - 1 in ring order. With net, the result is a randomization of net (see the module description): its nodes are placed on the ring in random order, and n and the number of edges (so k, the average degree) are those of net, unless k is given; p is always needed. In multilayer networks each layer is randomized on its own, with its own ring and number of edges (k can be one value for all layers or a dictionary from layer to average degree); interlayer edges are kept.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: not supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • n – Number of nodes; None with net.

  • k – Average degree; None with net, for its own.

  • p – Probability of rewiring each edge.

  • net – An undirected, not bipartite Network to randomize, or None.

  • rewire – "target", "both" or "add".

  • connected – Whether the graph must be connected (each layer, in multilayer networks).

  • tries – Maximum number of attempts to get a connected graph.

  • seed – Seed of the random numbers, or None for a random seed.

  • device – Device of the result, e.g. "cuda"; None for the CPU, or for the device of net.

Returns:

The small world graph, a new Network.

Raises:

ValueError – If p is missing or not between 0 and 1, if neither n and k nor net are given, if rewire is not valid, if net is directed or bipartite, if n or device differ from those of net, if there are more edges than node pairs, if connected is asked with k below 2 or no connected graph is found in tries attempts, or for dictionaries as in gnm_random_graph().

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

g = gsk.watts_strogatz_graph(1000, 6, 0.1, seed = 1)                  # rewire one endpoint
g = gsk.watts_strogatz_graph(1000, 6, 0.1, rewire = "both", connected = True)
g = gsk.watts_strogatz_graph(1000, 6, 0.1, rewire = "add")            # Newman-Watts shortcuts

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", "nodes.tsv")
rnd = gsk.watts_strogatz_graph(net = net, p = 0.05, seed = 1)          # same n and m, nodes on a ring
gsk.average_clustering(net), gsk.average_clustering(rnd)

Community benchmarks

Graphs with planted communities, the ground truth against which community discovery algorithms are tested: equal communities and degrees (Girvan-Newman), or broad degrees and community sizes, with directed, weighted and overlapping variants (LFR). The mixing parameter mu, the fraction of the edges of a node leaving its communities, sets how hard the communities are to find.

gn = gsk.girvan_newman_graph(0.3, seed = 1)                         # 4 x 32 nodes, degree 16, mu 0.3
rnd = gsk.girvan_newman_graph(net = net)                            # random equal communities, mu and degree of net

lfr = gsk.lfr_benchmark_graph(1000, 20, 50, 0.3, minc = 20, maxc = 100, seed = 1)
gsk.modularity(lfr, "community")                                    # quality of the planted communities
lfr = gsk.lfr_benchmark_graph(1000, 20, 50, 0.3, directed = True, muw = 0.2, on = 100, om = 2)
rnd = gsk.lfr_benchmark_graph(net = net, mu = 0.5)                  # degrees and communities of net, more mixed
graphoskop.girvan_newman_graph(mu=None, l=None, k=None, degree=None, net=None, directed=None, attribute='community', seed=None, device=None)

Girvan-Newman benchmark (Girvan and Newman, 2002) for community discovery: l communities of k nodes, and every node expects degree edges, a fraction mu of them (the mixing parameter) to nodes of other communities. Pairs of nodes are edges independently, with probability \(p_{in}\) within communities and \(p_{out}\) between them, so this is a planted_partition_graph() with equal communities, parametrized by how hard they are to find rather than by densities; it is also a caveman graph (see caveman_graph()) whose caves are thinned and mixed. The classic benchmark is the default: 128 nodes in 4 communities of 32, average degree 16 (expected external degree \(z_{out} = 16 \mu\)). Communities are found easily for small mu, and become undetectable as mu approaches \(1 - 1 / l\), where nodes have as many edges to each other community as to their own.

For n nodes in communities of sizes \(s_c\) and average degree \(d\) (out-degree if directed), \(p_{in} = n d (1 - \mu) / \sum_c s_c (s_c - 1)\) and \(p_{out} = n d \mu / (n^2 - \sum_c s_c^2)\), so that the average degree is degree and the expected fraction of edges between communities is mu. The graph has no self loops nor parallel edges, and is generated as planted_partition_graph(), in time about linear in the number of edges.

Without net, nodes are the integers 0 to n - 1, community by community. With net, the result is a randomization of net (see the module description): its nodes are assigned at random to l communities, whose sizes differ by at most one (l given, or n / k rounded if k is given), with the average degree of net. Without l and k, the number of communities is that of the node attribute attribute of net, and without mu the mixing parameter is the fraction of the edges of net between its communities (self loops left out); for communities of net that are kept as they are, use planted_partition_graph(). In multilayer networks each layer is randomized on its own, with its own communities (numbered across layers, so that no two layers share one), average degree and parameters (mu, l, k and degree can be dictionaries from layer to value); interlayer edges are kept.

In both cases the community of every node is stored as the numeric node attribute attribute (overwriting that of net), unless None: the planted partition, the ground truth with which to compare the communities found.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: not supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • mu – Mixing parameter, between 0 and 1; None with net, for its own.

  • l – Number of communities; None for 4 without net.

  • k – Number of nodes of each community; None for 32 without net.

  • degree – Average degree (out-degree if directed); None for 16 without net, or for the average degree of net.

LFR benchmark (Lancichinetti, Fortunato and Radicchi, 2008) for community discovery, with the variants of Lancichinetti and Fortunato (2009): directed, weighted, and with overlapping communities. Unlike girvan_newman_graph(), degrees and community sizes are broad, as in real networks: degrees follow a power law of exponent t1 with average k and maximum maxk, community sizes a power law of exponent t2 between minc and maxc, and every node has a fraction mu (the mixing parameter) of its edges to nodes outside its communities.

The graph is built as in the original: nodes are placed in communities by a random matching, then swaps until every node fits, i.e. its internal degree \((1 - \mu) k_i\) is smaller than the size of its community; each community is wired as a configuration model of the internal degrees, and the external degrees as a configuration model between nodes that share no community. A few hundred rounds of parallel repairs (see configuration_model()) remove self loops, parallel edges and external edges within communities; the few left are dropped, so some degrees can be a little lower than drawn, and, when small communities are almost cliques, mu a little higher (e.g. 0.303 for 0.3 with communities of 20 nodes and average degree 20). Internal degrees larger than the community allows (a hub that fits in no community) are cut to fit, the rest going outside. Everything runs on the device, in time about linear in the number of edges.

Variants:

  • directed (directed = True): in-degrees follow the power law and out-degrees are as equal as possible, as in the tests of Lancichinetti and Fortunato (2009); mu applies to both, and k and maxk are the average and largest in-degree;

  • weighted (muw given): node i has strength \(s_i = k_i^\beta\) (total degree if directed; beta 1.5 by default), a fraction muw of it on external edges, and weights are set to give every node its internal and external strengths as closely as possible (by iterative scaling, instead of the greedy minimization of the original). They are stored as the edge attribute weight;

  • overlapping communities (on above 0): on random nodes belong to om communities each, their internal degree split evenly among them; sizes add up to n + on (om - 1) memberships. Communities are then a qualitative node attribute of tuples (see Several values per node: overlapping communities) and modularity() is the overlapping one.

Without net, nodes are the integers 0 to n - 1, and the community of each node is stored as the node attribute attribute (unless None): numeric, communities numbered from the smallest, or tuples of them with overlaps. minc and maxc default to the smallest and largest degree, as in the original program.

With net, the result is a randomization of net (see the module description) with its degrees (in and out if directed) and its communities, read from the node attribute attribute (a list, tuple or set of communities for nodes in several), which it keeps: instead of drawing them from power laws, the benchmark uses those of net, and mu is the fraction of its edges (self loops left out) between nodes that share no community, unless given. If net has the edge attribute weight, the result is weighted: strengths are those of net (or \(k_i^\beta\) if beta is given) and muw the fraction of its weight on external edges, unless given; otherwise edge attributes are drawn from the old edges, as in the other generators. In multilayer networks each layer is randomized on its own (mu, muw and beta can be dictionaries from layer to value); interlayer edges are kept.

graphoskop.growing_manifold(n=None, d=None, p=0.0, net=None, seed=None, device=None, progress=None)

Growing discrete manifold: a \(d\)-dimensional simplicial complex grown one simplex at a time, in which every \((d - 1)\)-face is shared by at most two \(d\)-simplices (a pseudomanifold, see is_pseudomanifold()). It starts from one \(d\)-simplex; a face in a single \(d\)-simplex is free (incidence number 0), a face in two is saturated. At each step, a free face is drawn uniformly at random and:

  • with probability \(1 - p\), a new node joins it in a new \(d\)-simplex (the face becomes saturated): this alone is network geometry with flavor \(s = -1\) (Bianconi and Rahmede, 2016), which grows a \(d\)-ball;

  • with probability \(p\), the face is closed with a free face adjacent to it (sharing \(d - 1\) nodes), drawn uniformly at random among those for which the \(d\)-simplex spanned by the two is allowed: the two nodes not shared must not be linked (the closing move adds the link between them, as in Wu et al.), and none of the faces of the new simplex may be saturated. If there is none, nothing is added at that step. For \(d = 2\) this saturates two adjacent free links by completing their triangle with a new link between nodes at distance 2, the second move of Wu, Menichetti, Rahmede and Bianconi (2015), whose emergent geometry it generalizes; it creates cycles, so that the complex is no longer a ball.

Gluing moves grow the boundary (the free faces) and closing moves shrink it: with large p growth slows down, as most closing moves fail on small boundaries. The process stops when the complex has n nodes, or earlier, with a warning, if no free face is left (for \(d = 1\), a path closed into a cycle). The result is pure, with d-simplices as facets, and strongly connected.

The growth is sequential, in Python on the CPU: about 4 to 8 seconds per 100,000 nodes.

Simplicial complexes: supported; hypergraphs: not supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • n – Number of nodes, at least \(d + 1\) (with net, its number of nodes).

  • d – Dimension of the manifold, at least 1; None for 2 (triangles, the usual case), or with net for its dimension.

  • p – Probability of the closing move, in \([0, 1)\).

  • net – A simplicial complex to randomize: its nodes, with their attributes, placed in a random order of arrival; n is its number of nodes, and d its dimension unless given.

  • seed – Seed of the random numbers, or None for a random seed.

  • device – Device of the result.

  • progress – Whether to show a progress bar, or None for the global setting (see Progress bars).

Returns:

A simplicial complex (see Higher-order networks), nodes 0 to n - 1 in order of arrival.

Raises:

ValueError – If a parameter is not valid, or n is given with net.

Example:

graphoskop.hypergraph_configuration_model(degrees=None, sizes=None, net=None, mixing=10, repair=100, seed=None, device=None)

Hypergraph configuration model (Chodrow, 2020): a random hypergraph with given hyperedges per node (degrees) and hyperedge sizes, uniform over the hypergraphs with these sequences, a node being at most once in a hyperedge. graphoskop.simplicial_configuration_model(degrees=None, dimensions=None, net=None, mixing=10, repair=100, seed=None, device=None)

Simplicial configuration model (Young, Petri, Vaccarino and Patania, 2017): a random simplicial complex with given facets per node and facet dimensions. Every node has as many stubs as the facets it must belong to; every facet of dimension \(k\) has \(k + 1\) open stubs; stubs are matched at random. Neural generators

Neural generators: networks drawn from deep generative models trained on observed networks, which learn their structure (degrees, clustering, communities, motifs) without a formula for it.

graphrnn() trains GraphRNN (You, Ying, Ren, Hamilton and Leskovec, 2018), an autoregressive model that writes a graph node by node, and returns a GraphRNNFit, whose simulate() draws new networks, as simulate() does for exponential random graph models (so a fit is also a null model for randomization_test()).

fake = fit.simulate(seed = 2)[0]                           # as many generated molecules
test = gsk.randomization_test(molecules, gsk.average_clustering, fit, n = 20)

GraphRNN needs long training: the paper trains for 96,000 steps of 32 graphs, which takes hours even on a GPU. On two CPU threads, with a smaller model (hidden = 64, layers = 2), compared with the training graphs and with random graphs of the same numbers of nodes and edges:

  • 100 grids of 4 to 7 by 4 to 7 nodes: after 1,000 steps (111 s) the generated graphs are too large (38.1 nodes, against 31.9) and their degrees as far from the data as random graphs’ (distance of the degree distributions 0.42); after 4,000 steps (417 s), 30.8 nodes, mean degree 3.28 (data 3.29), degree distance 0.12, clustering 0.010 (data 0, random graphs 0.099);

  • 100 graphs of two dense communities (8 to 15 nodes each, density 0.5, one edge between them), 3,000 steps (270 s): 23.4 nodes (data 23.6), clustering 0.466 (data 0.479, random 0.247), modularity 0.400 (data 0.443, random 0.246); GraphRNN-S (110 s): 16.2 nodes, modularity 0.287;

  • one network of 8 cliques of 10 nodes in a ring (single network mode), 3,000 steps (634 s): mean degree 8.98 (9.00), clustering 0.874 (0.936), 65 nodes (80).

With the paper’s model (4 layers of 128) and only 1,500 steps, on grids of up to 12 by 12, nothing was learned yet (graphs of 2 nodes): give it many more steps, preferably on a GPU. On paths (to check the model), 1,000 steps give graphs of 19.5 nodes (data 20), 99% of them paths.

Memory does not grow with the size of the network (see graphoskop.graphrnn()): on single networks of 6,000 nodes and 30,000 edges, a random geometric graph (M = 338) and a preferential attachment network (M = 4,507), training takes about 3 GB, and on two CPU threads 13 and 22 seconds per step (a GPU is much faster). Generating a graph that large takes about n * M small steps. There, the loss of a step is on 6% (M = 338) or less of the nodes, which needs more steps: on the network of 8 cliques above, with the loss forced on 9% of the nodes, 3,000 steps give mean degree 7.38, clustering 0.631 and 37.4 nodes (against 8.98, 0.874 and 65 with the full loss; data 9.00, 0.936, 80).

graphoskop.graphrnn(net, dataset=None, simple=False, max_previous=None, steps=2000, batch_size=32, learning_rate=0.003, hidden=128, layers=4, embedding=64, edge_hidden=16, edge_layers=4, edge_embedding=8, seed=None, progress=None)

Train GraphRNN (You, Ying, Ren, Hamilton and Leskovec, 2018), a deep autoregressive generator of graphs, on a network, and return the fitted model, whose simulate() draws new networks like it.

A graph is written as a sequence: its nodes in the order of a breadth first search (from a random node, neighbors in random order), and for every new node its edges to the M nodes before it in that order (in a BFS order, the neighbors of a node are always close before it). A graph-level recurrent network (a GRU) reads the edges of every node and gives a state for the next one; from it, an edge-level GRU decides the edges of the new node one after the other, each depending on the ones before (simple = False, the full GraphRNN), or a small network gives all their probabilities at once, independently (simple = True, GraphRNN-S, faster, but weaker where edges depend on each other, as in communities). A node without any edge ends the graph, so the model also learns the sizes of the graphs (with independent edges, GraphRNN-S draws nodes without edges more often than it should, and its graphs tend to be too small). M (max_previous) is by default the largest distance, in random BFS orders of the training graphs, between the two ends of an edge.

The training graphs are the components of net (dataset = True: a network whose components are the graphs of a dataset, as in the paper), or its largest component alone, learned from many random BFS orders of it (dataset = False); with dataset = None, the components if none holds more than half of the nodes, the largest component otherwise. At every training step, batch_size training graphs are drawn at random, each in a new random BFS order (fewer if the graphs are large: at most 32,768 nodes in all, e.g. 5 orders of a network of 6,000 nodes), and the networks are trained on them with teacher forcing (binary cross entropy of the edges, Adam). The defaults are those of the paper (graph-level GRU of 4 layers of 128, edge-level of 4 layers of 16, learning rate 0.003). The network is used as undirected and unweighted (generated networks are undirected); attributes, node types and layers are ignored.

Training and sampling run on the device of the network. A sequence has one step per node, and every step up to M edges, so GraphRNN suits graphs of up to a few thousand nodes (the paper: up to 2,000): a single large network makes long sequences, with a large M if it has no narrow BFS order (random graphs and expanders have none, nor networks with hubs). To bound the memory, the graph-level GRU reads the edges in sparse form, and when the nodes of a batch have more than 524,288 edge positions (nodes times M), the loss of the step is computed on a random sample of its nodes (the graph-level GRU still reads all of them): an unbiased estimate, with the same expected gradient, which needs more steps. Training then takes about 3 GB whatever the size of the network (measured on networks of 6,000 nodes and 30,000 edges, with M from 300 to 4,500).

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: not supported (see Higher-order networks).

Configuration model (Molloy and Reed, 1995): a random graph with a given degree sequence.

Every node gets as many stubs (half edges) as its degree, and the stubs are matched uniformly at random, by one random permutation on the device. Random matching makes some self loops and parallel edges, rare unless some degrees are large compared with the square root of the number of edges. To avoid them without slowing generation down, a few rounds (rounds) of repairs follow, all in parallel: each self loop and each repeated edge trades endpoints with a random edge, keeping all degrees, when the trade creates neither a self loop nor an edge already there. The spurious edges still left (when the degrees leave no room, or after the last round) are then dropped, so that the degrees of their nodes are a little lower than asked, unless drop_self_loops or drop_parallel is False, in which case they are kept and degrees are exact. Most degree sequences need one or two rounds; for very heavy tails (degrees comparable with the number of nodes) more rounds leave fewer edges to drop, at a cost: a power law with exponent 2.2 on a million nodes drops 1.6% of its edges with 10 rounds (5 s on a CPU), 0.3% with 30 (10 s), 0.05% with 100 (27 s). With rounds = 0 and both kept, the graph is the classic configuration model multigraph, every matching of stubs equally likely; repairs make simple graphs slightly non-uniform.

degrees is a sequence with the degree of each node, nodes 0 to n - 1; for directed graphs, a pair (out_degrees, in_degrees), aligned, with the same sum; for bipartite graphs (directed false), a pair (degrees_0, degrees_1) with the degrees of the nodes of type 0 (0 to n0 - 1) and of type 1 (the next ones), with the same sum, whose edges always join nodes of different types. Undirected degrees must have an even sum.

With net, the result is a randomization of net (see the module description) with the degrees of its nodes (out- and in-degrees if directed; self loops count twice if undirected; parallel edges count), its node types if bipartite (edges only between types) and, in multilayer networks, each layer randomized on its own with the degrees of its intra-layer edges; interlayer edges are kept. Directed bipartite networks are not supported.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: not supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • degrees – Degree sequence, or a pair of them (see above); None with net.

  • net – A Network to randomize, or None.

  • directed – Whether the edges are directed; None for undirected, or for the direction of net.

  • rounds – Maximum number of repair rounds (0 for none).

  • drop_self_loops – Whether to drop the self loops left after the repairs.

  • drop_parallel – Whether to drop the parallel edges left after the repairs (one edge of each group stays).

  • seed – Seed of the random numbers, or None for a random seed.

  • device – Device of the result, e.g. "cuda"; None for the CPU, or for the device of net.

Returns:

The random graph, a new Network.

Raises:

ValueError – If neither degrees nor net are given, or both, if the degree sums do not allow a matching (odd, or different on the two sides), if directed or device differ from those of net, or if net is directed and bipartite.

Example:

graphoskop.edge_swap(net, swaps=None, seed=None, progress=None)

Edge swap randomization (Maslov and Sneppen, 2002): a randomization of net that keeps the degree of every node exactly (out- and in-degrees if directed, and node types if bipartite), by double edge swaps: two edges a - b and c - d become a - c and b - d (or a - d and b - c; a -> d and c -> b if directed), when this makes no self loop and no edge already there.

With many swaps the result is a uniformly random simple graph with the degrees of net, the null model of configuration_model() without its approximations (no dropped edges, no bias from repairs; for directed graphs swaps alone cannot reach every graph, e.g. the reversal of a directed triangle, so the result is uniform among the graphs they reach). With few swaps, it is a partial randomization: at most 2 swaps edges change (fewer, as some swaps are not valid), the others stay, so the number of swaps controls how much of the structure of net is destroyed (e.g. to follow how clustering or assortativity decay towards the null model). Every edge keeps its attributes (weight, sign) as it moves. Self loops and parallel edges of net are kept or swapped away, never created.

Swaps run in rounds, all at once on the device: every round pairs the edges at random and applies all the valid swaps that touch no node pair of another swap of the round, and the rare others one by one, so that the result is the same as that of the sequential Markov chain. swaps counts the swaps tried, valid or not: the uniform distribution needs a fixed number of steps of the chain (stopping after a number of successful swaps, as some libraries do, favors the graphs that allow more swaps). In multilayer networks each layer is swapped on its own (swaps can be a dictionary from layer to number); interlayer edges are kept.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: not supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • swaps – Number of swaps tried; None for 10 per edge, enough to randomize most networks (Milo et al., 2003). In multilayer networks, a number per layer or a dictionary.

  • seed – Seed of the random numbers, or None for a random seed.

  • progress – Whether to show a progress bar over the swaps (None: as set by set_progress(); see Progress bars); one bar per layer.

Returns:

The randomized network, a new Network.

Raises:

ValueError – For dictionaries as in gnm_random_graph().

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

gsk.average_clustering(net), gsk.average_clustering(part), gsk.average_clustering(rnd)
graphoskop.geometric_graph(n=None, r=None, m=None, d=2, positions=None, net=None, p=2, torus=False, position='position', seed=None, device=None)

Random geometric graph (Gilbert, 1961; Penrose, 2003): n nodes at random positions, uniformly in the unit cube of d dimensions, joined when they are close, within distance r; or, with m instead, joined by the m closest pairs, as if the radius grew until m pairs were within it. Geometric graphs model networks embedded in space (wireless and sensor networks, contacts between nearby individuals, road junctions): they have high clustering and long paths, and are the classic example of networks whose structure comes from a hidden metric space.

Close pairs are found on a grid of cells with side at least r: points are sorted by cell and each is compared with the points of its cell and of the adjacent ones only, all in parallel on the device, in time about linear in the number of nodes and edges. For m edges, the radius is first estimated from the expected density of pairs, grown if the boundaries left fewer than m pairs within it, and the m closest pairs are kept (ties broken arbitrarily). In more than 6 dimensions all pairs are compared. Distances are Minkowski distances of exponent p (2 for Euclidean, 1 for Manhattan, math.inf for the largest coordinate difference); with torus = True the unit cube wraps around, so that nodes near opposite sides are close (no boundary effects).

Without net, nodes are the integers 0 to n - 1, at random positions or at positions (an n by d array, in the unit cube if torus), which are stored as the numeric node attributes position_0, position_1, … (position is the prefix; None for no attributes). With net, the result is a randomization of net (see the module description) with its number of nodes and, unless r or m is given, its number of edges (pairs of nodes, self loops left out, at most all pairs): if net has the node attributes position_0, position_1, …, they are the positions (e.g. coordinates of real places, whose graph of closest pairs is then built), otherwise nodes get random positions, which are stored. In multilayer networks each layer is randomized on its own, with the positions of its copies; r and m can be dictionaries from layer to value. Interlayer edges are kept.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: not supported (see Higher-order networks).

  • device – Device of the result, e.g. "cuda"; None for the CPU, or for the device of net.

graphoskop.kronecker_graph(initiator=None, n=None, k=None, net=None, directed=None, self_loops=False, seed=None, device=None)

Kronecker graph (Leskovec, Chakrabarti, Kleinberg, Faloutsos and Ghahramani, 2010): the adjacency matrix is a Kronecker power of a small initiator matrix \(\Theta\), of \(N_1\) nodes. Repeating the product makes graphs that are self-similar at all scales, with heavy-tailed degrees, small diameter and community-within-community structure, from a handful of parameters.

With a 0/1 initiator, the graph is the deterministic Kronecker graph. With probabilities, it is a stochastic Kronecker graph: writing nodes u and v in base \(N_1\) with k digits, the edge (u, v) is there independently with probability \(p_{uv} = \prod_l \Theta_{u_l v_l}\). Pairs are drawn exactly with these probabilities by ball dropping (one initiator cell per digit) with a thinning correction, in time about linear in the number of edges, not of pairs; initiator values equal to 1 mixed with others make generation slower (up to 20 candidate pairs per edge). The Kronecker power is k, or the smallest one with at least n nodes, of which n are then kept at random. Self loops (pairs u = v, which have a probability too) are dropped unless self_loops; undirected graphs need a symmetric initiator.

Without net, nodes are the integers 0 to n - 1 (in the order of their Kronecker ids). With net, the result is a randomization of net (see the module description) with n its number of nodes and, unless given, a 2 by 2 initiator fitted to it by the method of moments of Gleich and Owen (2012): the one whose expected numbers of edges, wedges, 3-stars and triangles (if undirected; edges, out- and in-wedges, 2-paths and mutual pairs if directed) are closest to those of net, in relative terms (with three or four parameters for four or five counts, each is matched within a few percent, e.g. edges 5% fewer on average on a 3,000 node Kronecker graph). This is much faster than the maximum likelihood fit (KronFit) of Leskovec et al., but its nodes are not matched to those of net: the nodes of net take random positions. In multilayer networks each layer is randomized on its own, with its own initiator (initiator can be a dictionary from layer to matrix); interlayer edges are kept.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: not supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • initiator – N1 by N1 matrix (nested lists, array or tensor) of probabilities, or of 0 and 1; None with net, for one fitted to it.

  • n – Number of nodes; None with net or k.

  • k – Kronecker power, for N1 ** k nodes; None with n or net.

  • net – A Network to randomize, not bipartite, or None.

  • directed – Whether the edges are directed; None for undirected, or for the direction of net.

  • self_loops – Whether to keep self loops.

  • seed – Seed of the random numbers, or None for a random seed.

  • device – Device of the result, e.g. "cuda"; None for the CPU, or for the device of net.

Returns:

The Kronecker graph, a new Network.

Raises:

ValueError – If neither initiator with n or k nor net are given, if n or k are given with net, if the initiator is not a square matrix of probabilities, or not symmetric for an undirected graph, if n is not positive, if net is bipartite, if directed or device differ from those of net, or for dictionaries as in gnm_random_graph().

graphoskop.degree_corrected_block_model(sizes=None, degrees=None, edges=None, net=None, attribute='community', directed=None, exact=False, rounds=10, drop_self_loops=True, drop_parallel=True, seed=None, device=None)

Degree-corrected stochastic blockmodel (Karrer and Newman, 2011): a stochastic blockmodel in which nodes of the same block can have very different degrees, as in real communities with hubs and peripheral members.

edges[r][s] is the expected number of edges between blocks r and s (from r to s if directed; the diagonal counts the edges within each block), and degrees gives the expected degree of every node relative to the others of its block: each edge between r and s picks its endpoint in r with probability proportional to its degree within r, \(\theta_i = d_i / \sum_{j \in r} d_j\), and likewise in s. A node then expects \(\theta_i \kappa_r\) edges, \(\kappa_r\) being the edges of its block (twice those within), so degrees that add up to \(\kappa_r\) in each block are kept on average. The number of edges of each pair of blocks is Poisson, as in the original model, or exactly edges[r][s] (rounded) with exact = True. A block whose degrees are all 0 picks its nodes uniformly.

Drawing endpoints independently makes some self loops and parallel edges, mostly among hubs. As in configuration_model(), without slowing generation down, each of them is drawn again within its pair of blocks for up to rounds rounds, in parallel, and those left are dropped, unless drop_self_loops or drop_parallel is False; with rounds = 0 and both kept, the graph is the original multigraph model. Redrawing and dropping lower the degrees of hubs a little when they are large compared with the square root of the number of edges of their blocks. Everything runs on the device, in time about linear in the number of edges and of pairs of blocks.

Without net, nodes are the integers 0 to n - 1, block by block (sizes), with degrees in that order (a pair (out_degrees, in_degrees) if directed), and the block of each node is stored as the numeric node attribute attribute (unless None). With net, the result is a randomization of net (see the module description): its nodes keep their communities, read from the node attribute attribute (numeric or qualitative; every node needs a value), which give sizes; degrees are their degrees (out and in if directed) and edges the numbers of edges between communities, self loops left out, parallel edges counting, so that the result keeps both on average (exactly the numbers of edges between communities with exact = True, before drops). edges can be given instead, as a matrix over the communities in sorted order (or order of appearance, if their values cannot be sorted), or a dictionary from layer to matrix. In multilayer networks each layer is randomized on its own, with the degrees and edges of its intra-layer edges; interlayer edges are kept.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: not supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • sizes – Number of nodes of each block; None with net.

  • degrees – Expected degree of each node, block by block (a pair of out- and in-degrees if directed); None with net.

  • edges – k by k matrix of expected numbers of edges between blocks; None with net, for its own.

  • net – A Network to randomize, not bipartite, or None.

  • attribute – Name of the node attribute holding the block of each node: read from net, or written to the result without net (None for no attribute).

  • directed – Whether the edges are directed; None for undirected, or for the direction of net.

  • exact – Whether each pair of blocks gets exactly edges[r][s] edges (rounded) before drops, instead of a Poisson number.

  • rounds – Maximum number of rounds of redrawing self loops and parallel edges (0 for none).

  • drop_self_loops – Whether to drop the self loops left.

  • drop_parallel – Whether to drop the parallel edges left (one edge of each group stays).

  • seed – Seed of the random numbers, or None for a random seed.

  • device – Device of the result, e.g. "cuda"; None for the CPU, or for the device of net.

Returns:

The degree-corrected blockmodel graph, a new Network.

Raises:

ValueError – If neither sizes, degrees and edges nor net are given, if sizes or degrees are given with net, if degrees does not have one non-negative value per node, if edges is not a k by k non-negative matrix, symmetric for an undirected graph, if net is bipartite, has no node attribute attribute or a node without a value, if directed or device differ from those of net, or for dictionaries as in gnm_random_graph().

Example:

graphoskop.mixed_membership_block_model(probs=None, memberships=None, n=None, alpha=None, net=None, attribute='community', threshold=None, directed=None, seed=None, device=None)

Mixed membership stochastic blockmodel (Airoldi, Blei, Fienberg and Xing, 2008): every node belongs to several blocks (overlapping communities) with a vector of weights \(\pi_i\) adding up to 1, and every pair of nodes is an edge independently with probability \(p_{ij} = \pi_i^T B \pi_j = \sum_{a, b} \pi_{ia} B_{ab} \pi_{jb}\), B being the matrix probs of probabilities between blocks (stochastic_block_model() is the special case of nodes in one block each). This is the version of the model with fixed memberships, as in its inference; in the original generative story every pair of nodes draws a block for each node anew, which gives the same probabilities.

Pairs are drawn exactly with these probabilities, without going through all pairs of nodes: a Poisson process proposes candidate pairs block pair by block pair, with a rate a bit larger than the probability of every pair, and each candidate is kept with the probability that corrects the difference. Everything runs on the device, in time about linear in the number of edges, of memberships and of pairs of blocks. The graph has no self loops nor parallel edges. In undirected graphs probs must be symmetric; in directed graphs probs[a][b] is for edges from block a to block b.

Without net, nodes are the integers 0 to n - 1, with memberships, an n by k matrix of non-negative weights (normalized to add up to 1 in every row), or with weights drawn from a Dirichlet distribution of parameter alpha (a number for all blocks, or one per block; small values give nodes mostly in one block, large values nodes spread over all of them). The blocks of each node, those with weight at least threshold (by default 1 / (k + 1), so that a node spread evenly over any number of blocks is in all of them), are stored as the qualitative node attribute attribute (unless None): a tuple of blocks, or just the block for a node in one.

With net, the result is a randomization of net (see the module description): its nodes keep their communities, read from the node attribute attribute, in which a node in several communities has a list, tuple or set of them (see Several values per node: overlapping communities); each node weighs its communities equally. probs, unless given (as a matrix over the communities in sorted order, or order of appearance if their values cannot be sorted), is the method of moments estimate: the matrix with which the expected weight of the edges between every pair of communities, every edge counting \(\pi_{ia} \pi_{jb}\) for communities a and b, equals the one of net (self loops left out, parallel edges counting). For communities that do not overlap this is the density of the edges between them, as in stochastic_block_model(); values outside 0 and 1, possible when overlaps are not explained by the model, are clipped. In multilayer networks each layer is randomized on its own, with the communities of its copies and its own probabilities (or a dictionary from layer to matrix); interlayer edges are kept.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: not supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • probs – k by k matrix of probabilities between blocks (nested lists, array or tensor); None with net, for its own.

  • memberships – n by k matrix of membership weights, or None for n and alpha.

  • n – Number of nodes, with alpha.

  • alpha – Parameter of the Dirichlet distribution of the memberships: a positive number, or one per block.

  • net – A Network to randomize, not bipartite, or None.

  • attribute – Name of the node attribute holding the communities of each node: read from net, or written to the result without net (None for no attribute).

  • threshold – Without net, smallest weight of a block for a node to be listed in it; None for 1 / (k + 1).

  • directed – Whether the edges are directed; None for undirected, or for the direction of net.

  • seed – Seed of the random numbers, or None for a random seed.

  • device – Device of the result, e.g. "cuda"; None for the CPU, or for the device of net.

Returns:

The mixed membership blockmodel graph, a new Network.

Raises:

ValueError – If neither probs with memberships or n and alpha nor net are given, if memberships, n or alpha are given with net, if probs is not a k by k matrix of probabilities between 0 and 1, or not symmetric for an undirected graph, if memberships has negative values or a row of zeros, if alpha is not positive, if net is bipartite, has no node attribute attribute or a node without a value, if directed or device differ from those of net, or for dictionaries as in gnm_random_graph().

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

B = [[0.3, 0.01], [0.01, 0.3]]
g = gsk.mixed_membership_block_model(B, n = 200, alpha = 0.1, seed = 1)    # mostly in one block
g = gsk.mixed_membership_block_model(B, memberships = [[1, 0], [0.5, 0.5], [0, 1]])   # "community": 0, (0, 1), 1
gsk.modularity(g, "community")                                             # overlapping modularity

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", "nodes.tsv")     # "community": e.g. [0, 1] for a node in both
rnd = gsk.mixed_membership_block_model(net = net, seed = 1)
graphoskop.nested_block_model(n=None, m=None, levels=None, degrees=None, net=None, attribute='community', directed=None, rounds=10, drop_self_loops=True, drop_parallel=True, max_blocks=4096, seed=None, device=None)

Nested degree-corrected stochastic blockmodel (Peixoto, 2014, 2017): a degree-corrected blockmodel whose numbers of edges between groups are themselves generated by a blockmodel of the groups, and so on up a hierarchy of groups of groups, ending in a single group. It is the most general of the blockmodels here: communities, core-periphery or bipartite-like structures at several scales, and heterogeneous degrees, all generated from the numbers of groups of every level alone. It is also the model that fit_nested_block_model() fits, and that the functions of Network error correction use to reconstruct networks.

Without net, the network is drawn top-down from the model’s priors:

  • the partition of each level (the n nodes into levels[0] groups, those into levels[1] groups, …) is uniform given its group sizes, which are uniform among all compositions (every group non-empty);

  • the m edges are spread over the pairs of groups of the top level, and those of every pair of groups over the pairs of their subgroups one level down, down to the pairs of bottom groups, each time uniformly among all multisets (a Dirichlet-multinomial with all parameters 1, drawn by giving each edge a pair with probability proportional to Exp(1) weights of the pairs);

  • the edges of each bottom group are spread over its nodes, each half-edge picking a node in proportion to degrees (relative degrees within the group) or, by default, to Exp(1) weights, which makes the degree sequence of each group uniform among all those with its number of half-edges (the “uniform” degree prior of the microcanonical model; the description length of fit_nested_block_model() uses the more permissive prior of degree histograms).

The edges are then wired as in degree_corrected_block_model() with exact = True: the numbers of edges between bottom groups are exact, self loops and parallel edges redrawn for up to rounds rounds and those left dropped (unless asked otherwise). The model is a multigraph, and its priors are heavy-tailed: they often pile the edges of the network on a few pairs of groups, beyond what their pairs of nodes can hold, so that dropping can remove a good share of the m edges (a quarter, for 2,000 nodes, 10,000 edges and levels [32, 8, 2]); drop_parallel = False keeps the multigraph, with exactly m edges if self loops are kept too. Directed networks spread the edges over ordered pairs of groups, with out- and in-degrees. Everything runs on the device, in time and memory linear in the number of edges and of pairs of groups of every level. Nodes are the integers 0 to n - 1, bottom group by bottom group, and the group of every node at every level is stored as numeric node attributes: attribute for the bottom level, attribute + "_1", attribute + "_2", … for the levels above.

With net, the model is first fitted to net by fit_nested_block_model() (undirected networks only, not multilayer nor bipartite; parallel edges count once, self loops are ignored), and the result is a randomization of net with the fitted hierarchy: it keeps the nodes, the exact numbers of edges between bottom groups and, on average, the degrees, and gets the groups of all levels as node attributes. The fit runs on the CPU (Numba), the wiring on the device.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: not supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • n – Number of nodes; None with net.

  • m – Number of edges; None with net.

  • levels – Number of groups of every level, bottom first, non-increasing, at most n (e.g. [16, 4]: 16 groups of nodes, in 4 groups); None with net.

  • degrees – Relative degree of each node, bottom group by bottom group (a pair of out- and in-degrees if directed), or None for the uniform prior; None with net.

  • net – A Network to fit and randomize, or None.

  • attribute – Name of the node attribute of the bottom groups (None for no attributes).

  • directed – Whether the edges are directed; None for undirected. Must be undirected with net.

  • rounds – Maximum number of rounds of redrawing self loops and parallel edges (0 for none).

  • drop_self_loops – Whether to drop the self loops left.

  • drop_parallel – Whether to drop the parallel edges left (one edge of each group stays).

  • max_blocks – Maximum number of bottom groups of the fit (with net).

  • seed – Seed of the random numbers, or None for a random seed.

  • device – Device of the result, e.g. "cuda"; None for the CPU, or for the device of net.

Returns:

The nested blockmodel graph, a new Network.

Raises:

ValueError – If neither n, m and levels nor net are given, if they are given together, if levels is not a non-increasing list of positive numbers at most n, if degrees does not have one non-negative value per node, or if net is directed, multilayer or bipartite.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

g.get_node_attribute("community_1")                           # groups of the middle level

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv")
rnd = gsk.nested_block_model(net = net, seed = 1)              # fitted hierarchy of net
graphoskop.planted_partition_graph(sizes=None, p_in=None, p_out=None, net=None, attribute='community', directed=None, seed=None, device=None)

Planted partition graph, the classic stochastic blockmodel (Holland, Laskey and Leinhardt, 1983; Condon and Karp, 2001): nodes are split into communities of the given sizes, and every pair of nodes is an edge independently, with probability p_in if both are in the same community and p_out otherwise. With p_in > p_out communities are denser inside than between them (assortative), as in community detection benchmarks; with p_in < p_out the structure is disassortative.

Edges are drawn as two Gnp graphs (see gnp_random_graph()), within and between communities: a binomial number of edges, then a uniformly random set of that many pairs, on the device, in time about linear in the number of edges. The graph has no self loops nor parallel edges.

Without net, nodes are the integers 0 to n - 1, community by community, and the community of each node (its position in sizes) is stored as the numeric node attribute attribute (unless None), a planted partition for e.g. modularity(). With net, the result is a randomization of net (see the module description): its nodes keep their communities, read from the node attribute attribute (numeric or qualitative; every node needs a value), which give sizes; p_in and p_out are the densities of the edges of net within and between communities (self loops left out, parallel edges counting), unless given. In multilayer networks each layer is randomized on its own, with the communities of its copies and its own densities (p_in and p_out can be one value for all layers or a dictionary from layer to value); interlayer edges are kept.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: not supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • sizes – Number of nodes of each community; None with net.

  • p_in – Probability of an edge between two nodes of the same community; None with net, for its own.

  • p_out – Probability of an edge between two nodes of different communities; None with net, for its own.

  • net – A Network to randomize, not bipartite, or None.

  • attribute – Name of the node attribute holding the community of each node: read from net, or written to the result without net (None for no attribute).

  • directed – Whether the edges are directed (ordered pairs); None for undirected, or for the direction of net.

  • seed – Seed of the random numbers, or None for a random seed.

  • device – Device of the result, e.g. "cuda"; None for the CPU, or for the device of net.

Returns:

The planted partition graph, a new Network.

Raises:

ValueError – If neither sizes, p_in and p_out nor net are given, if sizes is given with net, if a probability is not between 0 and 1, if net is bipartite, has no node attribute attribute or a node without a value, if directed or device differ from those of net, or for dictionaries as in gnm_random_graph().

Example:

graphoskop.stochastic_block_model(sizes=None, probs=None, net=None, attribute='community', directed=None, seed=None, device=None)

Stochastic blockmodel (Holland, Laskey and Leinhardt, 1983): nodes are split into blocks (communities) of the given sizes, and every pair of nodes is an edge independently, with a probability that depends on their two blocks: probs[a][b] for a node of block a and one of block b. Any mixing pattern can be planted: assortative communities (large diagonal), disassortative or bipartite-like structures, core-periphery, hierarchies. planted_partition_graph() is the special case with one probability inside blocks and one between them.

Every pair of blocks is a Gnp graph (see gnp_random_graph()): a binomial number of edges, then a uniformly random set of that many pairs, all pairs of blocks at once on the device, in time about linear in the number of edges and in the number of pairs of blocks. The graph has no self loops nor parallel edges. In undirected graphs probs must be symmetric; in directed graphs probs[a][b] is the probability of an edge from block a to block b.

Without net, nodes are the integers 0 to n - 1, block by block, and the block of each node (its position in sizes) is stored as the numeric node attribute attribute (unless None). With net, the result is a randomization of net (see the module description): its nodes keep their communities, read from the node attribute attribute (numeric or qualitative; every node needs a value), which give sizes; probs is the density of the edges of net between each pair of communities (self loops left out, parallel edges counting), unless given, as a matrix over the communities in sorted order (or order of appearance, if their values cannot be sorted). In multilayer networks each layer is randomized on its own, with the communities of its copies and its own densities (a probs given applies to the communities present in each layer, or can be a dictionary from layer to matrix); interlayer edges are kept.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: not supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • sizes – Number of nodes of each block; None with net.

  • probs – k by k matrix of probabilities (nested lists, array or tensor); None with net, for its own.

  • net – A Network to randomize, not bipartite, or None.

  • attribute – Name of the node attribute holding the block of each node: read from net, or written to the result without net (None for no attribute).

  • directed – Whether the edges are directed; None for undirected, or for the direction of net.

  • seed – Seed of the random numbers, or None for a random seed.

  • device – Device of the result, e.g. "cuda"; None for the CPU, or for the device of net.

Returns:

The stochastic blockmodel graph, a new Network.

Raises:

ValueError – If neither sizes and probs nor net are given, if sizes is given with net, if probs is not a k by k matrix of probabilities between 0 and 1, or not symmetric for an undirected graph, if net is bipartite, has no node attribute attribute or a node without a value, if directed or device differ from those of net, or for dictionaries as in gnm_random_graph().

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

g = gsk.stochastic_block_model([50, 450], core_periphery, seed = 1)

rnd = gsk.stochastic_block_model(net = net, seed = 1)            # same densities between every pair of communities

graphoskop.randomization_test(net, statistic, model, n=100, seed=None, progress=None, **parameters)

Test a statistic of a network against a null model: the statistic is computed on net and on n randomizations of it made by model, and the observed value is compared with their distribution.

model is any generator of this module that takes net (e.g. configuration_model(), edge_swap(), stochastic_block_model(); for hypergraphs hypergraph_configuration_model(), for simplicial complexes simplicial_configuration_model() or growing_manifold()), called as model(net = net, seed = ..., **parameters), or a fitted ERGM (ERGMFit), whose networks are drawn with simulate(). statistic is any function of a network returning one number, such as average_clustering() or lambda g: gsk.assortativity(g, "age"). Node level statistics return a vector: test their global version (global_triangle_closure() rather than local_triangle_closure()) or a summary of them (lambda g: gsk.local_triangle_closure(g).nanmean(), nodes in no triangle being NaN).

The pseudo p-values are the shares of randomizations scoring above, below or equal to the observed value; p_value is the two sided one, \(\min(1, 2 \min(P(x \geq obs), P(x \leq obs)))\). The distribution is tabulated on values rounded to a scale that gives about 20 bins (integers are kept as they are when they are few).

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes are tested as networks are, with a generator that takes them and a statistic that does (e.g. lambda g: gsk.betti_numbers(g)[1]); a generator of plain networks refuses them.

Hypergraphs and simplicial complexes: supported (see Higher-order networks).

Parameters:

  • net – A Network, hypergraph or simplicial complex.

  • statistic – Function of a network returning a number.

  • model – Generator function, or fitted ERGMFit.

  • n – Number of randomizations.

  • seed – Seed of the random numbers (randomization k gets seed + k), or None.

  • progress – Whether to show a progress bar over the randomizations (None: as set by set_progress(); see Progress bars); the generator shows none.

  • parameters – Other arguments of the generator (e.g. swaps, p), or of simulate() (burnin, interval).

Returns:

Dictionary with observed (the value on net), samples (pandas.DataFrame with the value of every randomization), distribution (pandas.DataFrame with columns value and count, the rounded values), mean and std of the randomizations, z (standardized observed value), above, below and equal (shares of randomizations) and p_value.

Raises:

ValueError – If statistic does not return one number.

Example:
