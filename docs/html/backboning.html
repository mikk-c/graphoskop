Backboning

Network backboning: scoring edges against a null model, so that edges weaker than expected can be filtered out.

Scoring functions store their scores as new edge attributes of the Network (existing attributes with the same name are overwritten); computations run in double precision on the device of each component. extract_backbone() then keeps the edges whose score passes a threshold, test_bb_thresholds() summarizes the backbones given by a range of thresholds, and find_bb_thresholds() finds the thresholds at which the backbone changes qualitatively.

Scoring methods:

  • noise_corrected(): score and standard deviation, thresholded as a z-score.

  • disparity_filter(): score only.

  • doubly_stochastic(): score only.

  • high_salience_skeleton(): score only.

  • polya_filter(): score only.

spanning_tree(), maximally_filtered_graph() and triangulated_filtered_graph() instead filter the edges directly, keeping a maximum or minimum spanning tree, or a planar filtered graph, of every component.

The functions above take the name of the score attribute, prefix + "_score"; if the attribute prefix + "_sdev" exists, it is used as its standard deviation, otherwise the score is thresholded directly.

In multilayer networks every method works layer by layer, as if each layer were a separate network (the same as running it on layer_network() of each layer): only intra-layer edges are scored, with strengths, degrees, totals and paths of their own layer; couplings and other interlayer edges get NaN scores. extract_backbone(), spanning_tree(), maximally_filtered_graph() and triangulated_filtered_graph() always keep the interlayer edges between the copies they keep, and the statistics of test_bb_thresholds() and find_bb_thresholds() are computed on the intra-layer edges.

graphoskop.disparity_filter(net, weight='weight', policy='max', prefix='df')

Disparity filter backbone scores of all edges (Serrano, Boguna and Vespignani, 2009).

From the point of view of a node with k edges, the shares of its strength taken by its edges are compared with k uniformly random shares: an edge taking a share p gets the score \(1 - \alpha\), with \(\alpha = (1 - p)^{k - 1}\) the probability of a share at least as large. Nodes with a single edge give the score 0. The backbone keeps the edges with score > threshold, e.g. 0.95 for \(\alpha < 0.05\):

gsk.extract_backbone(net, 0.95, score = "df_score")

Every edge has two scores: from its source (its share of the out-strength, among the out-edges) and from its target (its share of the in-strength, among the in-edges). In undirected networks these are the shares of the strengths of its two endpoints. policy combines them:

  • "max": the edge passes if it is significant for at least one endpoint (as in the original paper);

  • "min": the edge passes only if it is significant for both endpoints;

  • "out" or "in" (directed networks only): the score from the source or from the target.

Parallel edges and self loops count as separate edges of their nodes (a self loop once in undirected networks). In multilayer networks, strengths and degrees count the intra-layer edges only, so they are those of each copy in its layer.

Parameters:

  • net – A Network, modified in place.

  • weight – Name of the edge attribute used as edge weight, or None for unit weights.

  • policy – "max", "min", "out" or "in".

  • prefix – The score is stored in the edge attribute prefix + "_score".

Raises:

ValueError – If policy is not valid for the network.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv")
gsk.disparity_filter(net)
backbone = gsk.extract_backbone(net, 0.95, score = "df_score")
graphoskop.doubly_stochastic(net, weight='weight', pseudocount=0.001, tol=1e-12, max_iter=1000, prefix='ds')

Doubly stochastic backbone scores of all edges (Slater, 2009).

The weighted adjacency matrix of each component, plus pseudocount in every cell, is scaled into a doubly stochastic matrix \(D_1 (A + \epsilon J) D_2\) (every row and column summing to 1); the score of an edge is its entry. The scaling of a positive matrix is unique. It is computed by Sinkhorn-Knopp iteration, which converges in a few iterations on most components, and, for the components not converged after 100 iterations (typically small, nearly decomposable ones, e.g. directed components with a small pseudocount), by the Newton method of Knight and Ruiz (2013) started from there. Components are scaled independently, so the scores of each component sum to its number of nodes. The pseudocount makes the matrix positive, which guarantees that the scaling exists; it is applied implicitly, so memory is linear in the number of edges and nodes. Parallel edges are summed into one cell and each gets its score; self loops are diagonal cells.

In multilayer networks, the matrices are those of the components of each layer (connected by intra-layer edges), as in layer_network().

The backbone keeps the edges with score > threshold. Scores scale with the inverse of the component size, so a threshold means different things in components of very different sizes. Slater’s second stage, adding edges by decreasing score until the network is connected, keeps the edges with a score of at least the smallest score of a maximum spanning forest: find_bb_thresholds() gives it as the smaller of first_isolate and first_component_break (keep the edges with a score above a threshold just below it).

Parameters:

  • net – A Network, modified in place.

  • weight – Name of the edge attribute used as edge weight, or None for unit weights.

  • pseudocount – Value added to every cell of each component’s matrix, in units of the weights. With 0 the scaling exists only for matrices with total support, and never if a node has no out- or in-edges.

  • tol – Convergence threshold: every row and column sum within tol of 1.

  • max_iter – Maximum number of Newton iterations after the Sinkhorn-Knopp ones; if reached, a warning is issued and the current scores are stored.

  • prefix – The score is stored in the edge attribute prefix + "_score".

Raises:

ValueError – If pseudocount is 0 and a node has no out- or in-edges.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv")
gsk.doubly_stochastic(net)
backbone = gsk.extract_backbone(net, 0.1, score = "ds_score")
graphoskop.extract_backbone(net, threshold, score='nc_score', keep_isolates=False, reconnect_components=False, reconnect_isolates=False)

Backbone of a network: the edges whose score is above a threshold.

If the score has a standard deviation (an attribute prefix + "_sdev" next to prefix + "_score", as stored by noise_corrected()), the edges with score > threshold * sdev are kept, threshold being the z-score (e.g. 1.28, 1.64 or 2.32 for one-sided p-values of 0.1, 0.05 and 0.01). Otherwise the edges with score > threshold are kept, for scores without a standard deviation or to threshold any edge attribute directly (e.g. the weight). Edges with a NaN score or standard deviation are dropped.

Dropped edges can be added back, ranked by the same criterion (score / sdev, or score; NaN ranks last):

  • reconnect_components: each original component split into p pieces (isolated nodes included) is joined again with p - 1 edges, the fewest possible, chosen as a maximum spanning forest of the pieces (Kruskal: repeatedly the most significant edge joining two different pieces). The backbone then has the same components, with the same nodes, as net, so this implies reconnect_isolates.

  • reconnect_isolates: each node left without edges gets back an edge, without rejoining larger pieces. Going from the most significant edge, an edge is added if one of its endpoints is still isolated, so at most one edge per isolated node and fewer when an edge joins two of them. Nodes without edges in net stay isolated.

Removing edges can split components, so the backbone is a new Network, with components recomputed and sorted by size; net is not modified. All edge and node attributes (numeric and qualitative) are kept; a bipartite network gives a bipartite backbone, with the same node types. In undirected networks both directions of an edge are kept or dropped together, according to the values of the input direction.

In multilayer networks only intra-layer edges are filtered, and reconnection works within each layer (its components and isolated copies). Interlayer edges (couplings included) are always kept between the copies that are kept; a copy left without intra-layer edges is dropped with its interlayer edges, unless keep_isolates. The backbone is multilayer, with the same layers.

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • threshold – Threshold.

  • score – Name of the edge attribute with the score.

  • keep_isolates – Keep nodes left without edges, as isolated components.

  • reconnect_components – Add back the fewest most significant dropped edges keeping every original component connected.

  • reconnect_isolates – Add back the most significant dropped edges of the nodes left without edges.

Returns:

The backbone, a new Network.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", directed = True)
gsk.noise_corrected(net)
backbone = gsk.extract_backbone(net, 1.64)                                  # z-score
backbone = gsk.extract_backbone(net, 1.64, reconnect_components = True)     # same components
strong = gsk.extract_backbone(net, 10, score = "weight")                    # weight above 10
graphoskop.find_bb_thresholds(net, target_degree=None, score='nc_score')

Thresholds at which the backbone changes qualitatively.

An edge is kept when its significance (score / sdev if the score has a standard deviation, as in extract_backbone(), otherwise score) is above the threshold, so every statistic of test_bb_thresholds() only changes at the significance of some edge. The thresholds below are found exactly among these values (no grid), then checked with the comparison of extract_backbone() and, if rounding of the scores makes it disagree, moved up by the smallest float32 steps until it agrees.

  • first_isolate: smallest threshold at which a node that has edges to other nodes in net is left without them; every smaller threshold keeps all such nodes.

  • first_component_break: smallest threshold at which the number of components with more than one node differs from net (a component splits into pieces with more than one node, or vanishes into isolated nodes). Nodes falling off a component without splitting it are reported by first_isolate.

  • target_degree: smallest threshold at which the average degree (as in test_bb_thresholds()) reaches target_degree, from above if it is lower than the average degree of net and from below otherwise.

A threshold is NaN if the change never happens, and -inf if it happens at every threshold (e.g. a node whose edges all have a NaN significance). Standard deviations are assumed positive.

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • target_degree – Target average degree, or None to skip that row.

  • score – Name of the edge attribute with the score (its standard deviation, if any, is found as in extract_backbone()).

Returns:

pandas.DataFrame with a criterion column and the columns of test_bb_thresholds(), one row per criterion.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", directed = True)
gsk.noise_corrected(net)
table = gsk.find_bb_thresholds(net, target_degree = 3)
backbone = gsk.extract_backbone(net, table.set_index("criterion").threshold["first_isolate"] - 0.01)
graphoskop.high_salience_skeleton(net, weight='weight', n_jobs=1, prefix='hss')

Link salience of all edges, for the high salience skeleton (Grady, Thiemann and Brockmann, 2012).

With effective distances \(1 / w\) (edges with a non-positive weight are not used), the shortest-path tree of a root is the union of all shortest paths from it to the other nodes (all tied paths included). The salience of an edge is the fraction of roots whose tree contains it, so it lies in \([0, 1]\); the high salience skeleton is made of the edges with salience close to 1, and salience is typically bimodal, so a threshold such as 0.5 works:

gsk.extract_backbone(net, 0.5, score = "hss_score")

The paper defines salience on connected undirected networks, normalized by the number of nodes. Here each component is normalized by its own number of nodes (the roots that can reach the edge at all), which is the same on a connected network. In directed networks trees follow the edge directions; in undirected networks an edge is in a tree if it is in either direction. Among parallel edges only the shortest ones can be on a shortest path; self loops never are. Paths whose lengths differ by less than one part in \(10^{10}\) count as tied, so that rounding in the sums of distances does not split ties.

In multilayer networks, trees use the intra-layer edges only, and each edge is normalized by the number of nodes of its connected piece within its layer (its component in layer_network()).

This needs a shortest-path search from every node, \(O(N E \log N)\), run by scipy.sparse.csgraph.dijkstra() on the CPU; the edges on each tree are then found on the device of the component. n_jobs processes split the roots of large components (checking edges on the CPU); on systems that start processes by spawning (Windows, macOS), the calling script must then be protected by if __name__ == "__main__":.

Parameters:

  • net – A Network, modified in place.

  • weight – Name of the edge attribute used as edge weight, or None for unit weights.

  • n_jobs – Number of processes.

  • prefix – The score is stored in the edge attribute prefix + "_score".

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv")
gsk.high_salience_skeleton(net)
skeleton = gsk.extract_backbone(net, 0.5, score = "hss_score")
graphoskop.maximally_filtered_graph(net, weight='weight', kind='maximum')

Planar maximally filtered graph (PMFG; Tumminello et al., 2005) of a network, as a new network: a planar subgraph with a large total weight.

Edges are taken from the largest weight to the smallest, and an edge is kept if the kept edges stay planar (they can be drawn on a plane without crossings), as Kruskal’s algorithm keeps an edge if no cycle is formed. The result contains the maximum spanning tree (see spanning_tree()) and keeps the same components, with the same nodes, as net; a component of \(n \geq 3\) nodes keeps at most \(3(n - 2)\) edges, exactly that many if it was a complete graph (it is then a triangulation, with only triangles and 4-cliques as cliques). Finding the planar subgraph of largest total weight is NP-hard: this greedy construction is the one of the paper, and it is exact for planar networks, which are returned whole.

As in spanning_tree(), edge directions are ignored when choosing the edges and kept in the result, only the best of parallel edges can be chosen, self loops never are, edges with a NaN weight come last, and ties are broken deterministically. All edge and node attributes (numeric and qualitative) are kept, and node types in bipartite networks. In multilayer networks each layer is filtered on its own (intra-layer edges only), and all interlayer edges are kept.

Runs on the CPU, with the planarity test of is_planar(). In dense networks most edges are dropped, each after a planarity test; these are shortened by testing first only the kept edges near the dropped one (a part with it that is not planar is enough), and edges likely to be kept are tested in runs. For a complete graph of \(n\) nodes this takes about 10 seconds for \(n = 200\) and 2 minutes for \(n = 500\) (the cost grows about as \(n^3\); triangulated_filtered_graph() is a fast alternative).

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • weight – Name of the edge attribute used as edge weight (e.g. a correlation or a backbone score), or None for unit weights (edges taken in input order).

  • kind – "maximum", or "minimum" to favor the smallest weights (e.g. distances).

Returns:

The PMFG, a new Network.

Raises:

ValueError – If kind is not valid.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("correlations.tsv")                   # complete graph, weight column
pmfg = gsk.maximally_filtered_graph(net)
pmfg.number_of_edges()                                       # 3 * (n - 2)
pmfg = gsk.maximally_filtered_graph(net, "distance", kind = "minimum")
graphoskop.noise_corrected(net, weight='weight', prefix='nc')

Noise-corrected backbone scores of all edges (Coscia and Neffke, 2017).

The weight \(n_{ij}\) of each edge is compared with its expectation given the total weights of its source and target, \(n_{i.}\) and \(n_{.j}\), and of the network, \(n_{..}\) (summed over all components). The score lies in \([-1, 1]\), positive when the edge is stronger than expected; its standard deviation comes from a Bayesian (beta-binomial) estimate of the variance of \(n_{ij}\). The backbone keeps the edges with score > delta * sdev, e.g. with delta 1.28, 1.64 or 2.32 (one-sided p-values of 0.1, 0.05 and 0.01).

In undirected networks every edge is stored in both directions, so \(n_{i.} = n_{.i}\) is the strength of the node and both directions get the same values. Self loops are scored too; they count once in the strength of their node. Edges whose source or target holds all the weight of the network get NaN.

In multilayer networks, the strengths and \(n_{..}\) are those of the layer of the edge (\(n_{i.l}\), \(n_{.jl}\), \(n_{..l}\)): each layer is scored on its own.

Parameters:

  • net – A Network, modified in place.

  • weight – Name of the edge attribute used as edge weight, or None for unit weights.

  • prefix – Prefix of the names of the two new edge attributes, prefix + "_score" and prefix + "_sdev".

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", directed = True)
gsk.noise_corrected(net)
keep = net.get_edge_attribute("nc_score") > 1.64 * net.get_edge_attribute("nc_sdev")
graphoskop.polya_filter(net, a=None, weight='weight', policy='max', exact_max=10000, prefix='pf', bonferroni=False, engine=None)

Pólya filter backbone scores of all edges (Marcaccioli and Livan, 2019).

From the point of view of a node with k edges and strength s (integer weights, read as counts), the null model distributes its s units of weight with a Pólya urn: an urn with one ball for the edge and k - 1 for the others, from which s balls are drawn, each drawn ball being put back with a more of its color. The parameter \(a > 0\) is the strength of the self-reinforcement (the rich get richer). The weight \(w\) of the edge then follows a beta-binomial distribution, with \(\alpha = 1 / a\) and \(\beta = (k - 1) / a\):

\[P(w \mid k, s, a) = \binom{s}{w} \frac{B\left(\frac{1}{a} + w, \frac{k - 1}{a} + s - w\right)}{B\left(\frac{1}{a}, \frac{k - 1}{a}\right)}\]

and the p-value of an edge is \(P(X \geq w)\), the probability of a weight at least as large. With \(a \to 0\) this is the binomial null model of uniform random allocation; with \(a = 1\) and large strengths it is the disparity filter (disparity_filter()); larger values of \(a\) explain more heterogeneous weights as reinforcement, so fewer edges are significant.

The score stored is \(1 - p\), as for disparity_filter(): higher scores are more significant, and the backbone at significance \(\alpha\) keeps the edges with score > 1 - alpha, with the same rule in extract_backbone(), test_bb_thresholds() and find_bb_thresholds():

gsk.polya_filter(net)
backbone = gsk.extract_backbone(net, 0.95, score = "pf_score")   # alpha = 0.05

The paper also applies a Bonferroni correction, p-value \(< \alpha / L\), with \(L\) the number of tests: two per edge (one from each endpoint) with policy "max" or "min", one with "out" or "in", counting the edges of the whole network (each undirected edge once), or of the layer in multilayer networks. bonferroni = True stores \(1 - L p\) instead, with the same thresholds (0.95 for \(\alpha = 0.05\)). Use it rather than the threshold \(1 - \alpha / L\) on the uncorrected score: scores are stored in single precision, which cannot tell apart values within about \(6 \cdot 10^{-8}\) of 1. The corrected score is not clipped: edges with \(L p > 1\) have negative scores, down to \(1 - L\), so all edges stay ordered by p-value. With \(L = 2E\) the correction is very strict: in networks with small weights few edges pass it.

Every edge has two p-values, from its source (its weight among the out-edges, out-strength and out-degree) and from its target (among the in-edges); in undirected networks, from its two endpoints. policy combines them as in disparity_filter(): "max" keeps the larger score (the edge passes if it is significant for at least one endpoint, as in the paper), "min" the smaller one, "out" or "in" (directed networks only) one of the two. Nodes with a single edge give the p-value 1, so such edges are judged by their other endpoint; edges of weight 0 have p-value 1.

Discrete and continuous model. In a network (layer, in multilayer networks) whose weights are all integers, they are read as counts and the model above is used as is. If any weight is not an integer, the whole network uses its large strength limit, where the share \(w / s\) follows the beta distribution \(B(\alpha, \beta)\), for the p-values and for the estimate of \(a\) (the beta-binomial with fractional draws would be meaningless).

Computation. With integer weights the p-value is an exact sum of beta-binomial probabilities, in double precision, on the shorter side of the distribution: \(P(X \geq w)\) (\(s - w + 1\) terms) or \(1 - P(X < w)\) (\(w\) terms). The second has an absolute error of about \(10^{-12}\), so p-values below \(10^{-6}\) are summed again on the upper tail, which far from the mean stops early (when a bound on the remaining terms falls below \(10^{-16}\) of the sum): relative errors are below \(10^{-6}\) down to the smallest p-values. Each term is obtained from the previous one by the ratio of consecutive probabilities. With engine = "numba" the sums run in a compiled kernel on the CPU, one edge per thread, about a nanosecond per term: on two CPU cores, a network with a million edges and geometric weights takes about 2 s with mean weight 20, 3 s with mean 200 and 13 s with mean 2000, plus 6 to 10 s to estimate \(a\). With engine = "torch" they run on the device of the network, 32 terms at a time for all the edges at once, in batches of about \(2^{22}\) terms (about 400 MB): memory bound, several times slower than Numba on a CPU, meant for GPUs. By default, the torch engine is used for networks on a GPU, the Numba engine otherwise. The maximum likelihood estimate runs on the device of the network with both.

Sums longer than exact_max terms, and continuous models, use instead the large strength limit of the model: the tail of the share \(w / s\) in the beta distribution \(B(\alpha, \beta)\) (on the CPU, with SciPy, for both engines). It is exact for the disparity filter (\(a = 1\) gives \((1 - w / s)^{k - 1}\)), and its own asymptotic form is the approximation the paper suggests for non-integer weights, which is less accurate. It is reliable only when \(s \gg k / a\) and \(w \gg 1\): at \(w = 1000\) its error is typically about 1% of the p-value, but it can reach a factor of 2 when \(s\) is not much larger than \(k / a\). Rescaling non-integer weights to integer counts makes them exact, but the model is not scale invariant: the p-values depend on the unit of the weights.

Choice of a. With a = None it is estimated by maximum likelihood (Appendix F of the paper), over \([10^{-3}, 10^3]\): the log-likelihood sums the log-probabilities of all the weights from the points of view used by policy (both endpoints for "max" and "min"), skipping nodes with a single edge; in the continuous model, the log-densities of the shares \(w / s\), skipping shares 0 and 1. A warning is issued if the estimate is at a boundary (e.g. unit weights, less heterogeneous than any urn in the range). Alternatively, \(a\) can be swept: backbones shrink as it grows. The paper’s other criteria (salience) are not implemented.

Parallel edges and self loops count as separate edges of their nodes (a self loop once in undirected networks), as in disparity_filter(). In multilayer networks every layer is filtered on its own: strengths and degrees count the intra-layer edges, and with a = None each layer gets its own estimate.

Parameters:

  • net – A Network with non-negative weights, modified in place.

  • a – Reinforcement parameter, positive, or None for the maximum likelihood estimate.

  • weight – Name of the edge attribute used as edge weight, or None for unit weights.

  • policy – "max", "min", "out" or "in".

  • exact_max – Maximum number of terms of an exact sum; longer sums use the beta approximation. 0 uses the approximation everywhere.

  • prefix – The score is stored in the edge attribute prefix + "_score".

  • bonferroni – Store one minus the Bonferroni corrected p-value, \(1 - L p\), instead of \(1 - p\).

  • engine – "numba" (CPU threads), "torch" (device of the network), or None.

Returns:

The value of \(a\) used: a float, or, in multilayer networks with a = None, a dict from layer to its estimate.

Raises:

ValueError – If policy or engine is not valid, if a is not positive, or if a weight is negative.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("flights.tsv")              # integer weights: passengers
a = gsk.polya_filter(net)                          # maximum likelihood a
backbone = gsk.extract_backbone(net, 0.95, score = "pf_score")
gsk.test_bb_thresholds(net, 0.9, 0.99, 0.01, score = "pf_score")
gsk.polya_filter(net, a = 1)                       # discrete disparity filter
gsk.polya_filter(net, a = a, bonferroni = True)    # as in the paper
graphoskop.spanning_tree(net, weight='weight', kind='maximum')

Maximum or minimum spanning tree of every component (a spanning forest), as a new network.

Unlike the scoring functions, this filters the edges directly: each component keeps n - 1 of its edges, connecting all its nodes with the largest (or smallest) total weight, so the result has the same components, with the same nodes, as net, which is not modified. All edge and node attributes (numeric and qualitative) are kept, and node types in bipartite networks. weight can be any edge attribute, e.g. a backbone score.

Edge directions are ignored when choosing the edges (the tree spans each weakly connected component) and kept in the result. In multilayer networks the trees span the components of each layer, made of intra-layer edges only, and all interlayer edges are kept. Among parallel edges only the best one can be chosen; self loops never are. Edges with a NaN weight are used last, only if needed to connect the component. Ties are broken arbitrarily but deterministically.

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • weight – Name of the edge attribute used as edge weight, or None for unit weights (any spanning tree).

  • kind – "maximum" or "minimum".

Returns:

The spanning forest, a new Network.

Raises:

ValueError – If kind is not valid.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv")
tree = gsk.spanning_tree(net)                                  # maximum weight
tree = gsk.spanning_tree(net, "distance", kind = "minimum")
tree = gsk.spanning_tree(net, "nc_score")                      # most significant edges
graphoskop.test_bb_thresholds(net, min_threshold, max_threshold, step, score='nc_score')

Size of the backbones given by a regular grid of thresholds, to help choosing one.

The thresholds go from min_threshold to max_threshold (included if on the grid) in steps of step, and edges are kept as in extract_backbone() (without reconnection). For each threshold the result reports, in absolute terms and as a percentage of the original network (the same statistic computed on net):

  • nodes: nodes that are not isolated, i.e. with at least one edge to another node;

  • edges: edges (in undirected networks, each counted once; self loops included);

  • avg_degree: average degree of the non-isolated nodes, \(2E / N\) in undirected and \(E / N\) (average out- or in-degree) in directed networks;

  • components: weakly connected components with more than one node.

Backbones are not built, so this is much faster than calling extract_backbone() for each threshold.

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • min_threshold – First threshold.

  • max_threshold – Last threshold.

  • step – Distance between consecutive thresholds, positive.

  • score – Name of the edge attribute with the score (its standard deviation, if any, is found as in extract_backbone()).

Returns:

pandas.DataFrame with one row per threshold and columns threshold, nodes, nodes_pct, edges, edges_pct, avg_degree, avg_degree_pct, components, components_pct.

Raises:

ValueError – If step is not positive or max_threshold < min_threshold.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", directed = True)
gsk.noise_corrected(net)
table = gsk.test_bb_thresholds(net, 1, 3, 0.25)   # z-scores 1, 1.25, ..., 3
graphoskop.triangulated_filtered_graph(net, weight='weight', kind='maximum')

Triangulated maximally filtered graph (TMFG; Massara et al., 2016) of a network, as a new network: a planar subgraph with a large total weight, much faster to build than maximally_filtered_graph().

Starting from the 4 nodes with the largest strength (sum of their weights above the mean weight), joined as a tetrahedron, the node joined with the largest total weight to the 3 nodes of a triangular face is repeatedly inserted in that face, which it splits into 3 new faces, until all nodes are in. The result is a maximal planar graph (a triangulation, \(3(n - 2)\) edges for \(n \geq 3\) nodes) made of 4-cliques glued on triangles, so it is chordal; it is usually close to the PMFG in total weight. Each step needs an arg-max over the faces and an update of the faces that had the inserted node as best candidate, all as tensor operations on the device of the network, so a GPU helps for large networks. On a CPU, a complete graph of \(n\) nodes takes about 0.4 seconds for \(n = 500\) (the PMFG takes minutes) and 13 seconds for \(n = 5000\). Ties are broken by node order.

The method is designed for complete graphs (e.g. correlation matrices), and works on a dense \(n \times n\) weight matrix of every component (memory grows as \(n^2\)). In sparse networks the pairs without an edge are used last (a node is inserted where it has the most edges to the face, then by weight); they are not in the result, which can then have fewer edges and more components than net. Components of 3 or fewer nodes are kept whole.

As in spanning_tree(), edge directions are ignored when choosing the edges and kept in the result, only the best of parallel edges can be chosen and self loops never are; edges with a NaN weight are treated as absent. All edge and node attributes (numeric and qualitative) are kept, and node types in bipartite networks. In multilayer networks each layer is filtered on its own (intra-layer edges only), and all interlayer edges are kept.

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • weight – Name of the edge attribute used as edge weight (e.g. a correlation or a backbone score), or None for unit weights.

  • kind – "maximum", or "minimum" to favor the smallest weights (e.g. distances).

Returns:

The TMFG, a new Network.

Raises:

ValueError – If kind is not valid.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("correlations.tsv", device = "cuda")  # complete graph, weight column
tmfg = gsk.triangulated_filtered_graph(net)
tmfg.number_of_edges()                                       # 3 * (n - 2)
