Network statistics

Simple network statistics: connected components, degree of nodes, degree distributions, average degree, power law fits of the degree distribution, degree centralization, density, reciprocity, average shortest path length, clustering coefficients, assortativity.

Nodes are given by their input id (a key of node_map) or by their internal id (component, index); a tuple that is both raises ValueError. Parallel edges are counted separately. A self loop adds 1 to the out- and the in-degree of its node, so 2 to its degree in undirected networks.

In multilayer networks only intra-layer edges count: couplings and other interlayer edges never do. The degree of a copy (entity, layer) is its degree within its layer; an entity can also be given on its own, and its degree is then the sum over its copies (the overlapping degree, parallel edges in different layers counting separately). The number of distinct neighbors of an entity across layers is its degree in aggregate_network().

Clustering coefficients count the triangles around nodes. Edges are taken as unweighted, parallel edges count once and self loops are ignored. For node \(i\), with \(A\) the adjacency matrix, \(d_i\) its total (in plus out) degree and \(d^\leftrightarrow_i\) its number of reciprocated edges, the local clustering coefficient is (Fagiolo, 2007)

\[C_i = \frac{[(A + A^T)^3]_{ii}}{2 \, [d_i (d_i - 1) - 2 d^\leftrightarrow_i]},\]

the fraction of the possible directed triangles around \(i\) that exist. In undirected networks it is the usual \(C_i = 2 T_i / (k_i (k_i - 1))\), with \(T_i\) the number of triangles through \(i\) and \(k_i\) its number of neighbors. The global clustering coefficient (transitivity) is the sum of the numerators over the sum of the denominators: in undirected networks, three times the number of triangles over the number of connected triples. The average clustering coefficient is the mean of the local ones.

Triangles are found without forming matrix powers: the pairs of adjacent nodes are oriented from lower to higher degree, and each triangle is found once by expanding the 2-paths along the orientation (the work grows with \(m^{3/2}\) at most, even with hubs). Paths are expanded in batches of batch_size, so memory stays bounded (about 50 bytes per path), and all the work runs on the device of the network.

In multilayer networks only intra-layer edges count, so triangles are within layers: the clustering of a copy is its clustering in its layer, and global and average clustering are given per layer.

Connected components

graphoskop.connected_components_count(net, type='weak')

Number of connected components.

Weakly connected components (type = "weak") are the components the network is split into (see Network), isolated nodes included (each is a component). Strongly connected components (type = "strong", for directed networks) are the largest sets of nodes with a directed path from every node to every other; each weakly connected component splits into one or more of them. In undirected networks the two coincide. In multilayer networks the components are those of the supra-graph, interlayer edges included.

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • type – "weak" or "strong".

Returns:

Number of components.

Raises:

ValueError – If type is not valid.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("follows.tsv", directed = True)
gsk.connected_components_count(net)                    # weakly connected
gsk.connected_components_count(net, type = "strong")
graphoskop.connected_components_nodes(net, type='weak')

Nodes of each connected component (see connected_components_count() for the types).

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • type – "weak" or "strong".

Returns:

List of arrays of input node ids, one per component, largest first (for weakly connected components, the same order as the components of the network, and the same as node_ids).

Raises:

ValueError – If type is not valid.

Example:

Degree

graphoskop.degree(net, node=None, distribution='count', direction='out', component=None, by='layer', weight=None)

Degree of nodes, or degree distribution.

With weight, the weighted degree (strength): each edge adds the value of that edge attribute instead of 1 (in undirected networks a self loop adds twice its value, as it adds 2 to the degree); NaN values make the degree NaN. Values are then floats, and the distribution lists every distinct value.

  • node given: the degree of that node, as an int (a float with weight).

  • node a list (or array) of nodes: a pandas.DataFrame with columns node (as given) and degree.

  • node = None: the degree distribution, as a pandas.DataFrame with column degree (the values that occur, increasing) and a column named as distribution: "count" (number of nodes with that degree), "pdf" (fraction of nodes with that degree) or "ccdf" (fraction of nodes with that degree or higher).

  • node = None and distribution = None: the degree of every node, as a vector over the nodes of the component (int64, float with weight), on the device of the network, or a list of them if component is None, as the centralities (e.g. to rank the nodes, see si_model()). In multilayer networks, the degree of each copy, counting intra-layer edges only.

In multilayer networks (see the module description), by chooses the distribution: "layer" gives the distribution of each layer, over its copies, in one table with a first column layer (pdf and ccdf normalized within each layer); "entity" gives the distribution of the degrees of the entities (sums over their copies). Pooling the copies of all layers into one distribution is not offered: it would mix layers, and count each entity once per layer.

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • node – Node (input id, internal id (component, index) or, in multilayer networks, entity), list of nodes, or None.

  • distribution – "count", "pdf", "ccdf", or None for the degree of every node; used when node is None.

  • direction – In directed networks, "out", "in" or "total" (in plus out) degree; ignored in undirected networks.

  • component – For the distribution or the vector of degrees, index of the component (0 is the largest), or None for the whole network; ignored when node is given. With by = "entity", only the copies in that component are summed.

  • by – In multilayer networks, "layer" or "entity"; ignored otherwise.

  • weight – Name of a numeric edge attribute for the weighted degree, or None for the number of edges.

Returns:

Degree, pandas.DataFrame, or vector (list of vectors) of degrees.

Raises:

  • KeyError – If a node or the edge attribute does not exist.

  • ValueError – If distribution, direction or by is not valid, or if a node is both an input id and an internal id.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", directed = True)
k = gsk.degree(net, "alice")                                # out-degree of a node
k = gsk.degree(net, (0, 5), direction = "in")               # by internal id
table = gsk.degree(net, ["alice", "bob"])                   # several nodes
ccdf = gsk.degree(net, distribution = "ccdf", direction = "total")
k = gsk.degree(net, distribution = None, component = 0)     # vector over component 0
s = gsk.degree(net, "alice", weight = "weight")             # weighted out-degree (strength)

net = gsk.load_network("multiplex.tsv", multilayer = True, coupling = "all")
k = gsk.degree(net, ("alice", "work"))                      # degree in layer "work"
k = gsk.degree(net, "alice")                                # summed over the layers
pdf = gsk.degree(net, distribution = "pdf")                 # one distribution per layer
pdf = gsk.degree(net, distribution = "pdf", by = "entity")
graphoskop.average_degree(net, direction='out', component=None, by='layer')

Average degree: the mean of the degrees of all nodes, as given by degree().

This is \(2m / n\) in undirected networks (each edge adds 1 to the degree of both its nodes) and \(m / n\) in directed networks for the out- or the in-degree (each edge adds 1 to one out-degree and one in-degree, so the two averages are equal), \(2m / n\) for the total degree. Here \(m\) counts edges as number_of_edges() does (parallel edges and self loops included) and \(n\) counts all nodes, isolated nodes included. NaN if there are no nodes.

In multilayer networks only intra-layer edges count, and by chooses the average: "layer" gives the average degree of the copies of each layer (\(2 m_l / n_l\) in undirected networks), "entity" the average of the degrees of the entities (sums over their copies, as in degree()).

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • direction – In directed networks, "out", "in" or "total"; ignored in undirected networks.

  • component – Index of the component (0 is the largest), or None for the whole network.

  • by – In multilayer networks, "layer" or "entity"; ignored otherwise.

Returns:

A float; with by = "layer" in multilayer networks, a pandas.DataFrame with columns layer and average_degree, one row per layer with copies.

Raises:

ValueError – If direction or by is not valid.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv")
gsk.average_degree(net)                          # 2 m / n
net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", directed = True)
gsk.average_degree(net)                          # m / n, out-degree (equal to in-degree)
gsk.average_degree(net, direction = "total")     # 2 m / n
graphoskop.degree_power_law(net, direction='out', component=None, layer=None, k_min=None, significance=0.1, ccdf=False)

Fit of a power law to the degree distribution, with the tests needed to decide whether it is one (Clauset, Shalizi and Newman, 2009).

Degrees are integers, so all distributions are discrete, fitted by maximum likelihood to the tail \(k \ge k_{min}\) (degree 0 is left out):

  1. Regime. \(k_{min}\) (the shift) is the smallest degree from which the distribution is closest to a power law: for each candidate (each degree value leaving at least 10 nodes in the tail), the exponent is fitted and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance between the tail and the fit is computed; the minimum wins. k_min fixes it instead. A shift exists when \(k_{min}\) is above the smallest degree. The tests below compare models on this tail.

  2. Exponential. Log-likelihood ratio test (Vuong) against a discrete exponential, over all positive degrees and over the tail. If the exponential is significantly better over all degrees and the tail does not significantly favor the power law, the distribution is not a power law: no exponent is given (NaN) and a warning is issued, and the best fit returned is the exponential over all positive degrees. Both tests are needed: on exponential data, the search of step 1 can settle on a short far tail where a steep power law looks acceptable and the test lacks power; while a power law tail above a different body (a shift) must not be refused because of its body. If only the tail test is inconclusive, a warning is issued and the fit goes on.

  3. Lognormal. Same test against a discrete lognormal. Unless the power law is significantly better, a warning is issued (a lognormal and a power law are hard to tell apart on finite data), and the fit goes on.

  4. Cutoff. Nested likelihood ratio test against a power law with exponential cutoff, \(p(k) \propto k^{-\alpha} e^{-\lambda k}\). If the cutoff is significantly better, the exponent and \(\lambda\) of that fit are returned; otherwise the pure power law exponent, with \(\lambda = 0\).

A test is significant when its p-value is below significance (0.1 as in Clauset et al.). The goodness-of-fit p-value of Clauset et al. (by bootstrap) is not computed. Weighted degrees are not integers, so they are not supported.

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • direction – In directed networks, "out", "in" or "total" degree.

  • component – Index of the component (0 is the largest), or None for the whole network.

  • layer – In multilayer networks, a layer (its copies’ degrees), or None for the degrees of the entities (sums over their copies, as degree() with by = "entity").

  • k_min – Smallest degree of the tail, or None to choose it as above.

  • significance – Threshold on the p-values of the tests.

  • ccdf – Also return the fitted complementary cumulative distribution (see below).

Returns:

Dictionary describing the best fit and the tests behind it:

  • verdict: "power law", "power law with exponential cutoff" or "not a power law";

  • model: the best fit, "power law", "power law with exponential cutoff" or "exponential" (when the verdict is "not a power law");

  • exponent: \(\alpha\) (NaN for the exponential); cutoff: \(\lambda\) of the power law with cutoff (0 otherwise); rate: \(\lambda\) of the exponential (only then);

  • k_min: smallest degree of the fitted range (for the exponential, the smallest positive degree);

  • normalization: \(Z\), the sum of the unnormalized model over the fitted range (\(\zeta(\alpha, k_{min})\) for the power law, \(\sum_{j \ge k_{min}} j^{-\alpha} e^{-\lambda j}\) with cutoff, \(e^{-\lambda k_{min}} / (1 - e^{-\lambda})\) for the exponential);

  • tail_fraction: \(f\), the fraction of the nodes considered (degree 0 included) with degree at least k_min;

  • formula: the fitted distribution written out, \(P(k)\) being the fraction of the nodes with degree \(k\), e.g. "P(k) = 0.2647 * k^(-2.368) / 0.025817, for k >= 12";

  • n (nodes with positive degree), n_tail (nodes in the fitted range), ks (Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance of the power law on its tail, NaN for the exponential);

  • tests: pandas.DataFrame, one row per test: alternative, degrees ("all" or "tail"), loglikelihood_ratio (positive favors the power law), p and favors;

  • with ccdf = True, ccdf: pandas.DataFrame with one row per degree value from k_min: degree, ccdf (fraction of nodes with that degree or higher, as degree() with distribution = "ccdf") and fit (the same from the best fit). Plotting both columns on log-log axes shows the fit over the empirical distribution.

Raises:

ValueError – If fewer than 10 nodes are in the tail, or a parameter is not valid.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv")
fit = gsk.degree_power_law(net)
fit["exponent"], fit["k_min"], fit["cutoff"]
fit["formula"]       # e.g. "P(k) = 0.2647 * k^(-2.368) / 0.025817, for k >= 12"
fit["tests"]
fit = gsk.degree_power_law(net, ccdf = True)
fit["ccdf"].plot(x = "degree", y = ["ccdf", "fit"], loglog = True)
graphoskop.degree_centralization(net, component=None, mode='all')

Degree centralization from the entropy of the degree sequence, adjusted for the size and density of the network.

The degree share entropy (the first degree-based entropy of Cambie and Mazzamurro, 2022), \(H = -\sum_i \frac{k_i}{T} \log \frac{k_i}{T}\) with \(T = \sum_i k_i\), is \(\log n\) when all nodes have the same degree and decreases as the edges concentrate on fewer nodes. It also grows with the number of nodes, so it is compared with its expected value \(E[H]\) in uniform random graphs with the same numbers of nodes \(n\) and edges \(m\) (Erdos-Renyi \(G(n, m)\)). There the degrees always sum to \(T\), so \(H = \log T - \frac{1}{T} \sum_i k_i \log k_i\) and \(E[H] = \log T - \frac{n}{T} E[k \log k]\), exactly, with the degree \(k\) of a node hypergeometric: a node takes part in \(n - 1\) of the \(n (n - 1) / 2\) possible edges (\(n (n - 1)\) in directed networks, \(2 (n - 1)\) per node with mode = "all"), \(m\) of which are drawn. No sampling is needed.

The centralization is \(C = 1 - e^{H - E[H]}\): \(e^H\) is the effective number of nodes sharing the edges, so \(C\) is the fraction of effective nodes lost with respect to a random network of the same size and density. It is 0 for degrees as even as at random, tends to 1 as the edges concentrate on a few hubs (a star has \(C = 0.74\) with 101 nodes, 0.97 with 10,001), and is negative for networks more even than random (e.g. a cycle). Networks of the same kind and different sizes have similar values.

For connected undirected networks with \(n - 1 \leq m \leq 2n - 3\) (sparse networks: trees up to average degree about 4), the smallest possible entropy \(H_{min}\) is known (Cambie and Mazzamurro, 2022): a node joined to all others plus a second hub. The relative centralization \(C' = (E[H] - H) / (E[H] - H_{min})\) then places the network between random (0) and the most centralized network with its numbers of nodes and edges (1). Since that range widens with \(\log n\) while networks of the same kind stay at a similar distance from random, \(C'\) decreases with size and is meant to compare networks of similar size; outside that range (directed, disconnected or denser networks) it is NaN.

Degrees are those of the simple graph: parallel edges count once, self loops are ignored, and weights are not used (the closed form holds for unweighted graphs only). Nodes without edges count in \(n\). In multilayer networks the supra-graph is used.

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • component – Index of the component (0 is the largest), or None for the whole network.

  • mode – In directed networks, the degree used: "all" (in plus out), "in" or "out"; ignored in undirected networks.

Returns:

Dictionary with "centralization" (\(C\)), "relative_centralization" (\(C'\)), "entropy" (\(H\)), "expected_entropy" (\(E[H]\)) and "minimum_entropy" (\(H_{min}\), NaN when unknown); all NaN without edges.

Raises:

ValueError – If mode is not valid.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv")
gsk.degree_centralization(net)["centralization"]
gsk.degree_centralization(net, component = 0)             # largest component, with C'

Density

graphoskop.density(net, component=None, by='layer')

Density: the fraction of the possible edges that are present.

  • undirected networks: \(2m / (n (n - 1))\);

  • directed networks: \(m / (n (n - 1))\) (both directions are possible for every pair);

  • bipartite networks: \(m / (n_0 n_1)\), or \(m / (2 n_0 n_1)\) if directed, with \(n_0\) and \(n_1\) the numbers of nodes of the two types (edges are only possible between types).

\(m\) counts the distinct pairs of nodes joined by an edge (distinct ordered pairs in directed networks): parallel edges count once and self loops are left out, so the density is between 0 and 1 (NetworkX instead counts every edge, self loops included). \(n\) counts all nodes, isolated nodes included. NaN if no edge is possible.

In multilayer networks only intra-layer edges count: couplings and other interlayer edges are left out, and the possible edges are those within each layer. by = "layer" gives the density of each layer, over its copies; by = "network" pools the layers, as the fraction of all the possible intra-layer edges, \(\sum_l m_l / \sum_l \binom{n_l}{2}\) in undirected networks.

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • component – Index of the component (0 is the largest), or None for the whole network.

  • by – In multilayer networks, "layer" or "network"; ignored otherwise.

Returns:

A float; with by = "layer" in multilayer networks, a pandas.DataFrame with columns layer and density, one row per layer with copies.

Raises:

ValueError – If by is not valid.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

gsk.density(gsk.load_network("edges.tsv"))                         # 2 m / (n (n - 1))
gsk.density(gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", directed = True))        # m / (n (n - 1))
net = gsk.load_network("multiplex.tsv", multilayer = True, coupling = "all")
gsk.density(net)                                                   # one density per layer
gsk.density(net, by = "network")                                   # all layers pooled

Reciprocity

graphoskop.reciprocity(net, normalized=False, component=None, by='layer')

Reciprocity of a directed network: the fraction of edges whose reverse edge is also present, \(r = L^\leftrightarrow / L\).

\(L\) counts the distinct ordered pairs joined by an edge (parallel edges count once, self loops are left out, as in density()), and \(L^\leftrightarrow\) those whose reverse pair is joined too. With normalized = True, the measure of Garlaschelli and Loffredo (2004), \(\rho = (r - a) / (1 - a)\) with \(a\) the density (density()): 0 if edges are reciprocated as often as by chance, positive if more often, negative if less, so that networks of different density can be compared. NaN if there are no edges (or, normalized, if the density is 1).

In multilayer networks only intra-layer edges count; by = "layer" gives the reciprocity of each layer, by = "network" pools the edges of all layers (and their densities, as in density()).

Parameters:

  • net – A directed Network.

  • normalized – Return \(\rho\) instead of \(r\).

  • component – Index of the component (0 is the largest), or None for the whole network.

  • by – In multilayer networks, "layer" or "network"; ignored otherwise.

Returns:

A float; with by = "layer" in multilayer networks, a pandas.DataFrame with columns layer and reciprocity, one row per layer with edges.

Raises:

ValueError – If the network is undirected (where every edge is reciprocated), or by is not valid.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("follows.tsv", directed = True)
gsk.reciprocity(net)                       # fraction of reciprocated edges
gsk.reciprocity(net, normalized = True)    # compared with the density

Average shortest path length

graphoskop.average_shortest_path_length(net, component=0, weight=None, inverse=False, samples=None, seed=0, n_jobs=None, lengths=None)

Average shortest path length: the mean length of the shortest paths between all ordered pairs of distinct nodes connected by a path.

Paths are those of shortest_paths (same engine, same weight and inverse options, shortest of parallel edges, directions followed in directed networks, the supra-graph in multilayer networks, where changing layer along a coupling is a step and pairs of copies of the same entity count). Pairs without a path are left out: nodes in different components, and, in directed networks, pairs where the target cannot be reached from the source (NetworkX instead raises an error unless the network is strongly connected). In an undirected component every pair counts, so this is the usual \(\frac{1}{n (n - 1)} \sum_{s \ne t} d(s, t)\). NaN if no pair is connected.

Lengths from each batch of sources are summed as soon as they are found, so memory stays at a few rows of lengths, not \(n^2\); searches are split across processes as in all_pairs_shortest_path_lengths() (see shortest_paths for n_jobs and the if __name__ == "__main__": guard). On large networks, samples uses only that many random source nodes (drawn uniformly from the nodes considered, seed for reproducibility), which estimates the average with \(O(\text{samples} \cdot m)\) work.

With lengths, the lengths precomputed by all_pairs_shortest_path_lengths() (with the same component, weight and inverse) are used and no path is searched again; weight and inverse are then ignored. The sums run on their device.

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • component – Index of the component (0 is the largest), or None for the whole network (pairs in different components left out).

  • weight – Name of the edge attribute used as edge length, or None for unit lengths.

  • inverse – Use the reciprocal of the edge attribute as length.

  • samples – Number of random source nodes, or None for all nodes (exact).

  • seed – Seed of the random sources.

  • n_jobs – Number of processes for the searches, None for all CPUs.

  • lengths – Precomputed lengths: a tensor for one component, or the list of tensors of all_pairs_shortest_path_lengths(net, None, ...) for component = None.

Returns:

The average length, a float.

Raises:

ValueError – If some lengths are negative or NaN, or lengths does not match.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv")
gsk.average_shortest_path_length(net)                                   # largest component
gsk.average_shortest_path_length(net, weight = "weight", inverse = True)
gsk.average_shortest_path_length(net, samples = 1000)                   # estimate
P = gsk.all_pairs_shortest_path_lengths(net)
gsk.average_shortest_path_length(net, lengths = P)                      # no new search

if __name__ == "__main__":   # needed for parallel searches on some platforms
   gsk.average_shortest_path_length(big_net, n_jobs = 8)

Assortativity

gsk.assortativity(net)              # degree assortativity
gsk.assortativity(net, "age")       # numeric attribute: Pearson correlation over the edges
gsk.assortativity(net, "city")      # qualitative attribute: Newman's categorical coefficient
graphoskop.assortativity(net, attribute=None, component=None, weight=None, direction=('out', 'in'))

Assortativity: the tendency of nodes to connect to nodes with similar attribute values (Newman, 2002 and 2003).

The kind of assortativity follows the type of the attribute:

  • numeric attribute: the Pearson correlation between the values at the two ends of the edges;

  • qualitative attribute: \(r = (\sum_i e_{ii} - \sum_i a_i b_i) / (1 - \sum_i a_i b_i)\), with \(e_{ij}\) the fraction of edges from a node of category \(i\) to one of category \(j\), \(a_i = \sum_j e_{ij}\) and \(b_j = \sum_i e_{ij}\);

  • no attribute (attribute = None): degree assortativity, the Pearson correlation between the degrees at the two ends of the edges, computed from the edges without any node attribute.

Both range from -1 (disassortative) to 1 (assortative). In undirected networks every edge counts in both directions, so the result is symmetric; in directed networks the value at the source is compared with the value at the target, and for degree assortativity direction chooses which degree is taken at each end (default: out-degree of the source, in-degree of the target). A self loop counts as much as any other edge (as in Newman’s formula and igraph; NetworkX counts self loops of undirected networks half), and degrees count them as degree() does. Edges with a missing value (NaN) at either end are left out; in long node tables, nodes without a numeric value have 0, as in get_node_attribute(). With weight, edges count proportionally to that edge attribute. NaN if undefined (no edges, or no variance).

In multilayer networks only intra-layer edges count, degrees are within layers (as in degree()), and the result is given per layer.

All the work runs on the device of the network, in one pass over the edges.

Parameters:

  • net – A Network.

  • attribute – Node attribute name (numeric or qualitative), or None for degree assortativity.

  • component – Index of the component (0 is the largest), or None for the whole network.

  • weight – Edge attribute used as weight, or None for unit weights.

  • direction – For degree assortativity in directed networks, the degree at the source and at the target: each "out", "in" or "total".

Returns:

A float; in multilayer networks, a pandas.DataFrame with columns layer and assortativity, one row per layer with edges.

Raises:

  • KeyError – If there is no such node or edge attribute.

  • ValueError – If direction is not valid.

Example:

import graphoskop as gsk

net = gsk.load_network("edges.tsv", "nodes.tsv")
gsk.assortativity(net)                       # degree
gsk.assortativity(net, "age")                # numeric attribute: Pearson
gsk.assortativity(net, "city")               # qualitative attribute: categorical
gsk.assortativity(net, weight = "weight")
